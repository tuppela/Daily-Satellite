# Brief: Envisat

Angle: The biggest civilian Earth observer ever launched stopped answering one Sunday, looked perfectly healthy, and is still up there because nobody ever worked out, or paid for, how to get it down.

Form: A vigil. Open on the silence at the ground station, follow the weeks of unanswered commands, then widen into the long afterlife: the removal studies that were funded as studies and never as removals. One tangent in the middle (the cellar appliance), one short line at the end that returns to "overdue for retirement".

Weights: history 40, technology 20, culture 10, reflection 30.

Entry: 8 April 2012, a ground station at Kiruna, Sweden. The satellite passes over and does not reply.

Technology to explain: One instrument only, GOMOS. As Envisat orbits, a star appears to sink toward the edge of the atmosphere and vanish behind it. Starlight passing through air loses particular wavelengths, and each gas removes its own, so the missing colours say which molecules are there (ozone in particular). Image: a barcode of absent colours. Mention the others in one line.

Culture (light): Papers and data: about 2,500 publications, Arctic sea-ice retreat among the uses.

Humour: ESA's own phrase "well overdue for retirement". A satellite that looks fine and will not answer. The ESTEC full-size model taken apart by cherry picker because the wind made it dangerous, while the real one stays up. Studying a removal feels like dealing with it.

Reflection: Designed for a world that had not yet decided to care about disposal. Money for technology studies, none for removal. Admit the narrator finds that restful. Say the unsaid thing: a thing studied feels nearly dealt with.

Ending: "Well overdue for retirement, ESA said. It has retired. It just hasn't left." Do not give a re-entry date.

Dark comedy dose: 3 (several dark turns, admissions about the silent-but-healthy satellite and about studying vs doing, tangent: appliance in a cellar bigger than the door, callback: "overdue for retirement").

Length: about 830 words.

## Fact sheet

Mission and size
- Envisat was launched on 1 March 2002 on an Ariane 5 from French Guiana. [ESA Earth Online mission page, https://dragon3.esa.int/eogateway/missions/envisat]
- ESA describes it, "at eight tonnes", as "the largest civilian Earth observation mission ever launched". [ESA Earth Online, same page]
- In orbit its size is given as about 26 m x 10 m x 5 m. [Guinness World Records "Largest Earth-observation satellite"; SpaceNews/Space.com 26 July 2010; DLR EOC Envisat page]
- It carried ten instruments: AATSR, ASAR, DORIS, GOMOS, LRR, MERIS, MIPAS, MWR, RA-2, SCIAMACHY. Others cover sea surface temperature (AATSR), radar imaging (ASAR), ocean and land colour (MERIS). [ESA Earth Online]
- It was designed for five years and was planned and designed in 1987 to 1990. [AFP via phys.org, April 2012; ESA "Focus on space debris Envisat", 11 Oct 2012]
- ESA said it had "already operated for double its planned lifetime" and was "well overdue for retirement". [ESA press release 9 May 2012, via ScienceDaily and Space.com 9 May 2012]

GOMOS
- As Envisat orbits, a background star appears to drop toward the edge of the atmosphere and disappear behind it. Starlight passing through the air loses certain wavelengths, and the blocked wavelengths are representative of the molecules and atoms in the atmosphere. GOMOS uses this to study ozone and tracks about 400 stars setting each day. [ESA, "Setting stars reveal planetary secrets", 5 Nov 2007]

Use of the data
- Over a thousand terabytes of data delivered; an estimated 2,500 scientific publications based on its data; uses included tracking Arctic sea-ice decline. [ESA press release 9 May 2012 via ScienceDaily]

Loss of contact
- The satellite did not respond as it passed over the ground station at Kiruna, Sweden; contact was lost on 8 April 2012, "just weeks after celebrating its tenth year in orbit". [AFP via phys.org, April 2012; Space.com; ESA press release 9 May 2012]
- ESA announced the loss on 12 April 2012 and declared the end of the mission on 9 May 2012. [Space.com 9 May 2012; AFP/phys.org]
- Engineers spent about a month sending commands from a widespread network of ground stations with no response. Ground radar and France's Pleiades satellite were used to look at it; images indicated the solar array was properly deployed. [ESA press release 9 May 2012; Space.com 9 May 2012; Space.com 24 April 2012]
- ESA's suspected scenarios: loss of the power regulator, or a short circuit triggering safe mode possibly followed by a second anomaly. [ESA press release 9 May 2012]
- Wikipedia's Envisat page does not state a confirmed cause. [Wikipedia, as fetched]

Why it could not be brought down
- Lowering Envisat to a re-entry-compliant orbit "was never an option because of its design and limited amount of fuel", even straight after launch. [ESA "Focus on space debris Envisat", 11 Oct 2012]
- ESA says deorbiting is now considered when developing new satellites and operating current ones, where technically feasible. [same]

Removal studies
- e.Deorbit was an ESA proposed debris removal mission with Envisat as target. In 2013 ESA issued an invitation to tender for a Phase A study. [ESA Clean Space blog, L. Innocenti, 26 Nov 2018; Wikipedia E.Deorbit]
- As of January 2016 ESA's page described e.Deorbit as due for launch in 2021, to rendezvous with Envisat, secure it and bring it down for a controlled re-entry. [ESA image page "Preparing to dismantle Envisat model", 6 Jan 2016]
- 26 November 2018: ESA's Clean Space head wrote "So, today, we have abandoned pushing for the specific e.Deorbit mission" and "The funding to continue the mission has not been provided." [ESA Clean Space blog, 26 Nov 2018]
- A statement reported by Space.com on 29 January 2019 quoted Luisa Innocenti: "Today we have the funding to develop relevant technologies but not to actually remove a defunct satellite." [Space.com, 29 Jan 2019]
- ClearSpace-1 is the follow-on mission and does not target Envisat; its target is PROBA-1 (April 2024 change). [Wikipedia ClearSpace-1, as fetched]

Model at ESTEC
- A full-size Envisat model stood at Space Expo, the visitor centre of ESA's ESTEC technical centre, from July 1999. Years of North Sea wind exposure made it a danger to the public and it was dismantled with a cherry picker and cutting torches (work on 18 December, article dated 6 January 2016). [ESA image page, 6 Jan 2016]

Status
- In January 2016 ESA described the real Envisat as having "drifted in an orbit about 800 km high". [ESA image page, 6 Jan 2016]
- In 2010 debris experts at the COSPAR congress said, given its orbit and area-to-mass ratio, it would take about 150 years to drift into the atmosphere. [SpaceNews/Space.com, 26 July 2010]
- Space.com (29 Jan 2019) called it now considered one of the most dangerous pieces of debris in orbit.
- No dated 2025 or 2026 source was reachable confirming it is still in orbit; the catalogue says "still orbiting". Orbit-tracking sites were blocked.

## Disputed or not to be used
- Mass: 8,211 kg (Wikipedia launch mass), about 8,000 kg (press), 8,100 kg and 7,911 kg dry (Guinness two entries). Use "about eight tonnes" as ESA does.
- Cost: US$2.9 billion (Space.com), US$3.4 billion (Spaceflight Now), EUR 2.5 billion (Wikipedia). Do not use.
- Length: 10.5 m (AFP), 9 m body (Space.com), 26 m with array (Guinness, DLR). Use only 26 x 10 x 5.
- Altitude: 782 km (2010), 768 km after 2010 manoeuvre (ESA 2012), "about 800" (ESA 2016). Use only "about 800 km in 2016".
- The 150-year figure is a 2010 conference estimate, not a re-entry date. Never state a date.
- Bus comparison, collision probabilities, the Chinese rocket stage near miss (2010): single secondary source, not used.
- Cause of failure: not confirmed anywhere found. Warhaut's "double failure on power bus more likely" is one ESA operations head's view (Spaceflight Now 2012); not used.
- Wikipedia "Kessler cascade from 10 kg object" and "21st of 50 concerning objects": not used.
- Blocked or unreachable: nbcnews, keeptrack.space, in-the-sky.org, celestrak, esa.int/Enabling_Support end-of-mission page, Kids ESA page.
