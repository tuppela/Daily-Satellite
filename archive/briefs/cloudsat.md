# Brief: CloudSat

Angle: A radar that looks straight down through clouds, kept alive for years past its plan by a working day cut short to protect a failing battery. The satellite that was demoted to short hours and carried on.

Form: A career told as a working life. Open on the battery fault of April 2011, explain the radar, step out to the clouds-and-shapes tangent, then follow the job: the line it flew in, the emergency exit, the short days, the move to its own train, the retirement.

Weights: technology 30, history 40, reflection 20, culture 10.

Entry: 17 April 2011. The first low-voltage fault, then emergency mode within a day.

Technology to explain: A 94 GHz radar pointed straight down. A 3.3 microsecond pulse; echo time gives depth, in 500 m layers; echo strength gives how much water or ice. Dragged along the orbit, it reads a cloud top to bottom, a column at a time. Exact image: the pulse is so short that a radio wave travels about a kilometre in that time (down and back gives 500 m layers).

Culture (light): Looking up at clouds and seeing rabbits (tangent). The A-Train named for other satellites' initials; the C-Train named after themselves.

Humour: A battery that cannot work nights. The sensible response to a small fault. Working short days and calling it commitment.

Reflection: Nobody retires a thing the day it gets worse; they cut its hours. Planned for 22 months, ran almost 18 years.

Ending: Quiet. Looked straight down for almost eighteen years; its orbit now leads the same way. No claim of re-entry.

Dark comedy dose: 3 (several dark turns; admissions about sensible faults, the short days and commitment; one tangent, clouds and rabbits; callback on "not interested in the rabbit" and on "straight down"; the unsaid paragraph about taking the short days).

Length: about 800 words.

## Fact sheet

Status
- Radar science operations ended in December 2023 (NASA science page: 20 December 2023; Wikipedia: radar deactivated 15 December 2023 UTC; the two differ, so the story says only "December 2023"). [https://science.nasa.gov/missions/cloudsat/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CloudSat]
- NASA, 23 April 2024: CloudSat has ended operations, was decommissioned in March 2024, and "was lowered into an orbit last month that will result in its eventual disintegration in the atmosphere." No re-entry date is given. [https://www.nasa.gov/missions/cloudsat/nasas-cloudsat-ends-mission-peering-into-the-heart-of-clouds/ ; https://www.space.com/nasa-ends-cloudsat-earth-observing-mission]
- BAE Systems (reported by Space Daily): after December 2023 it was passivated, which involved depleting residual energy and repositioning its solar arrays so it cannot reactivate, and is transitioning to a disposal orbit where it will eventually disintegrate on re-entering the atmosphere. [https://www.spacedaily.com/reports/CloudSat_satellite_completes_17_year_weather_research_mission_999.html]
- NASA science page: passivation April 2024. Wikipedia: last contact 23 May 2024. Neither gives a re-entry. No source found stating that re-entry has occurred. [https://science.nasa.gov/missions/cloudsat/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CloudSat]
- Mission duration: originally planned 22 months, design life 3 years; operated "almost 18 years" (NASA); Wikipedia gives 17 years, 11 months, 25 days. [NASA April 2024 article; https://atrain.nasa.gov/publications/CloudSat.pdf via web.archive; Wikipedia]

The instrument
- One instrument, the Cloud Profiling Radar: nadir-pointing, 94 GHz (W-band), "the first of its kind in space". [https://science.nasa.gov/missions/cloudsat/]
- Pulse length 3.3 microseconds, 500 m vertical resolution; footprint about 1.4 km by 1.8 km on board processing; it sends a pulse and records the echo; measures backscattered power as a function of distance. [CloudSat overview, https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20201218135936/https:/atrain.nasa.gov/publications/CloudSat.pdf ; CloudSat Data Users Handbook]
- Computed, not sourced: light travels about 3 x 10^8 m/s, so in 3.3 microseconds a wave travels about 1 km, which is 500 m each way. (General physics.)
- Cloud reflectivity rises with frequency; 94 GHz chosen as a compromise because higher frequencies are attenuated too much. [CloudSat overview]
- NASA: "A thousand times more sensitive than typical ground-based weather radars"; 3D vertical slices of clouds; let scientists see clouds and precipitation together; measured how often clouds produce rain and snow globally and how clouds heat and cool the atmosphere. [NASA April 2024 article]
- Orbit at launch: sun-synchronous, 98.2 degrees, 99-minute period, 705 km. [NASA science page]

The A-Train and C-Train
- Flew in the Afternoon Constellation (A-Train) with CALIPSO and others; satellites cross the equator "within seconds to minutes of each other" at about 1:30 pm local solar time. Never more than 6 satellites at one time; seven in total over the years. [https://science.nasa.gov/mission/a-train/ ; Hillger et al. reprint, https://www.engr.colostate.edu/~hillger/pdf/The_A-train_satellite_constellation_reprint.pdf]
- Nickname: "A-Train" because satellite names starting with "A" led and trailed the group at the time. [https://science.nasa.gov/mission/a-train/]
- CloudSat "12.5+/-2.5 seconds ahead of CALIPSO" in a document describing the formation as it was before 2011 (a later figure was not found). [EUMETSAT/Kidder, https://www-cdn.eumetsat.int/files/2020-04/pdf_conf_p50_s1_05_kidder_v.pdf]
- NASA: CloudSat and CALIPSO's "overlapping radar-lidar footprint" studies thin and thick clouds; CALIPSO uses laser light. [NASA April 2024 article; https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/news/sister-satellites-briefly-separated-working-together-again]
- 22 February 2018: CloudSat exited the A-Train, lowering its orbit by 10.5 km. NASA/Earthdata call the cause "a mechanical challenge" and do not name it. [https://science.nasa.gov/missions/cloudsat/ ; Earthdata]
- CALIPSO began leaving on 13 September 2018 and reached the new orbit on 20 September. The pair became the C-Train, "C being the first letter of each satellite". [Earthdata]

The 2011 battery anomaly
- Late 2009: soft short in one battery cell, a first sign of aging. Capacity was still enough for science; ground contacts were limited to sunlit periods. [Witkowski et al. paper, https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA568401.pdf]
- 17 April 2011: first under-voltage fault; within 24 hours it passed through the remaining levels into Emergency Mode. [same paper]
- The battery could supply only about 10% of the energy it had supplied a few days earlier. [same paper]
- CloudSat could not stay within its control box and was drifting toward Aqua; it needed to leave the A-Train; maneuvers were risky with so little power. Initial recovery attempts failed. [same paper; https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20130009146]
- The team sent the full manoeuvre sequence in a single 10-minute ground contact; the orbit was lowered and the spacecraft returned to safe mode within one sunlit period of 65 minutes. [same paper; NTRS abstract]
- About 65 minutes of sunlight and 34 minutes of eclipse per revolution at about 705 km. [same paper]
- Daylight Only Operations (DO-Op): payload and subsystems are cycled off in eclipse and on in sunlight so the battery never carries the full load; the radar is fully operational 9.5 minutes after eclipse exit. [same paper]
- NASA/JPL declared the satellite fully operational in November 2011. [same paper; NTRS abstract]
- Daytime-only operations lasted from 2011 to 2023. [Wikipedia]
- NASA 2024: engineers kept it running by "hibernating" the spacecraft during the night portions of each orbit and by orienting it with fewer reaction wheels. [NASA April 2024 article]

People (not used by name in the story)
- Graeme Stephens, principal investigator, JPL. Deborah Vane, project manager, JPL; said of the anomalies: "We recovered from these anomalies with techniques that no one has ever used before." [NASA April 2024 article]
- Spacecraft built by BAE Systems (formerly Ball Aerospace) of Broomfield, Colorado. [NASA April 2024 article; Wikipedia says Ball Aerospace]

## Disputed or not to be used

- Catalogue says "four other satellites, 15 seconds apart": not supported as a standing description. The A-Train held up to six satellites at once (so up to five others), and the CloudSat to CALIPSO gap is cited as 12.5 +/- 2.5 seconds. CloudSat left the A-Train in 2018. Not used.
- Catalogue says "has been looking straight down at clouds since 2006": true until December 2023, then the radar was switched off. Not used in the present tense.
- Radar off date: 15 December 2023 (Wikipedia, UTC) versus 20 December 2023 (NASA). Story says "December 2023".
- Decommissioning: March 2024 (NASA April 2024 article), passivation April 2024 (NASA science page), last contact 23 May 2024 (Wikipedia). Story says only the lowered orbit in March 2024.
- Whether CloudSat has re-entered: no source found. Do not say it has or has not; story says no source found.
- Cause of the 2018 problem: NASA says only a mechanical challenge or technical issues. Not named.
- Root cause of the 2011 battery failure: the paper mentions corrosion of the positive electrode; the story does not claim a cause.
- Wikipedia says "since the radar needed sunlight"; the engineering paper says the battery was the limit. Story follows the paper.
- A planned return to the A-Train (88 seconds behind CALIPSO) appears in the 2011 paper; whether and when it returned was not verified beyond Hillger's statement that it was in the A-Train until February 2018. Not used.
