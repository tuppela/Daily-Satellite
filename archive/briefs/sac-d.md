# Brief: Aquarius / SAC-D

Angle: Measuring the saltiness of the sea from orbit is an exercise in subtraction. The signal is a pinch of salt in a gallon, and everything else (waves, sunlight, atmosphere, human radio noise) has to be taken away first.

Form: A subtraction, worked in steps. Start with the faint glow of the sea, name the size of the salt signal, then subtract one disturbance at a time (land and atmosphere, sunlight, human interference, waves via the added radar). One tangent on tasting soup sits in the middle of the sum and returns to the dish. Then results, then the end of the mission, the status today.

Weights: technology 45, history 25, reflection 20, culture 10.

Entry: The sea is glowing, in microwaves, and salty water looks very slightly cooler than fresh.

Technology to explain: Passive L-band radiometry. Everything warm radiates; the sea radiates microwaves at about 1.4 GHz; the brightness temperature (in kelvin) depends on salinity, so saltier water looks slightly cooler. The signal is about 0.1 K for a change of about two parts in ten thousand. Three radiometers share one 2.5 m dish and watch a swath 390 km wide. The big error is sea roughness, so a radar scatterometer looks at the same patch, rougher sea scatters more, and that is subtracted. Exact physical image: a pinch of salt in a gallon of water (NASA's own analogy for 0.2 PSU), plus the stove ring that glows.

Culture (light): the spoon test on soup, as the human version of the same measurement without any corrections.

Humour: Human interference leaks into a band kept for radio astronomy. Wentz's funhouse mirror. The "first" that comes with a footnote. The project manager with no time for champagne. Dark turns come from the tiny signal being the narrator's opinions, and from the mission ending with nothing, "much easier to read".

Reflection: Most endings are one small unit stopping. The sea does not need the measurement.

Ending: The sea goes on glowing, a little dimmer where saltier, and nobody up there is reading it.

Dark comedy dose: 3 (several dark turns, admissions: opinions as tiny signal and "I would have found the time", one tangent: soup, one callback: tiny signal in a noisy environment becomes "nothing, which is much easier to read").

Length: about 800 words.

## Fact sheet

Status and the catalogue claim
- Aquarius/SAC-D is not operating. The mission ended on 8 June 2015 when a key part of the power and attitude control system stopped working. [NASA/JPL, "International Spacecraft Carrying NASA's Aquarius Instrument Ends Operations", https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/international-spacecraft-carrying-nasas-aquarius-instrument-ends-operations/ ; NASA news item dated 17 June 2015, https://salinity.oceansciences.org/news-more.htm?id=51]
- Preliminary indications were that a Remote Terminal Unit (RTU), an onboard hardware component, shut down, causing loss of onboard power regulation and spacecraft attitude stabilisation. [same NASA items; Via Satellite, 18 June 2015, https://www.satellitetoday.com/government-military/2015/06/18/power-and-attitude-control-system-failure-ends-sac-d-mission/]
- WMO OSCAR lists SAC-D as "Inactive", with end of life 7 June 2015; all instruments inactive since end of life (page last updated 5 January 2017). [https://space.oscar.wmo.int/satellites/view/sac_d]
- Orbit trackers still list NORAD 37673 in orbit: Orbital Radar, TLE epoch 2026-10-29, lists it as "Active" (a tracker label, not a mission status) at about 633 km, with an estimated orbital lifetime of 10 to 25 years before re-entry; isstracker.pl, epoch about 2026-10-28, lists about 645 km and no decay. [https://orbitalradar.com/satellite/37673 ; https://isstracker.pl/en/satellites/37673 ; both read 30 October 2026]
- Wikipedia gives orbital elements from 17 December 2015 (perigee 652.2 km, apogee 654.7 km, inclination 98.0 degrees, period 97.8 minutes) and no decay. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SAC-D]
- The catalogue says "first time from space". NASA says Aquarius gave "the first sea-surface salinity data ever collected by NASA" (first-light image) and was "NASA's first global observations of sea surface salinity". [https://aquarius.oceansciences.org/cgi/ov_mission.htm ; https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/missions/aquarius/] The Aquarius team's own history says the first attempt to measure ocean surface salinity from space was on Skylab in 1973 at 1.4 GHz. [https://aquarius.oceansciences.org/cgi/ov_mission.htm] ESA's SMOS launched 2 November 2009 [https://visuals.earth.esa.int/satellites/smos] and ESA released a first global map of soil moisture and sea-surface salinity on 29 October 2010, from August 2010 data, with quasi-global accuracy of 0.4 PSU. [https://www.esa.int/ESA_Multimedia/Images/2010/10/First_global_map_of_soil_moisture_and_ocean_salinity] A 2011 NASA article says ESA launched a soil moisture and ocean salinity mission in 2009. [https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/for-aquarius-sampling-seas-no-grain-of-salt-task/]

Mission and partners
- Launched 10 June 2011 on a Delta II from Vandenberg Air Force Base, California. [NASA/JPL, above; https://aquarius.oceansciences.org/cgi/ov_mission.htm]
- NASA and Argentina's space agency CONAE collaboration, with Brazil, Canada, France and Italy taking part. CONAE provided the spacecraft, built by INVAP (Argentina), and the mission operations centre. [NASA/JPL above; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SAC-D]
- Aquarius instrument built jointly by JPL and Goddard Space Flight Center. Goddard built and calibrated the radiometers; JPL built the scatterometer. [NASA/JPL above; https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130214000134/http://science.nasa.gov/missions/aquarius]
- Began mission mode 25 August 2011; data collection ran until 7 June 2015. [https://aquarius.oceansciences.org/cgi/ov_mission.htm ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SAC-D]
- Completed its primary three-year mission in November 2014. Gary Lagerloef, principal investigator, said the sensor "collected three years and nine months of valuable data". [NASA/JPL news, above]
- The first global map: within about two and a half weeks of operation the team produced a map showing more detail than expected [https://essic.umd.edu/nasas-salt-mapping-instrument-celebrates-first-year-in-orbit]; first-light global image released 22 September 2011. [https://aquarius.oceansciences.org/cgi/ov_mission.htm]
- The first map showed a larger-than-expected area of low-salinity Amazon outflow, and a sharp contrast between the high-salinity Arabian Sea and the low-salinity Bay of Bengal, which is influenced by the Ganges and monsoon rains. [https://m.phys.org/news/2011-09-aquarius-yields-nasa-global-ocean.html]
- Gene Feldman, project manager at NASA Goddard, on the first telemetry: "The first time the thing chirped, we got the data and posted it on the Web." On the celebration: "We didn't get to pop champagne - we didn't have the time!" (Paraphrased in the story.) [https://www.spacedaily.com/reports/NASA_Ocean_Salinity_Pathfinder_Celebrates_its_First_Year_in_Orbit_999.html]
- Before Aquarius, salinity was measured only by ships, buoys and floats, described as "still few and far between". [https://www.spacewar.com/reports/For_Aquarius_Sampling_Seas_No_Grain_of_Salt_Task_999.html ; JPL original]

How it measured
- Three passive microwave radiometers at L-band, 1.41 GHz (1.413 GHz in the JPL science writers' guide), measured the natural microwave emission of the sea surface as brightness temperature in kelvin. [https://aquarius.oceansciences.org/cgi/data_measurement.htm ; https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/press_kits/Aquarius_SAC-DScienceWritersGuide.pdf]
- "Everything radiates energy," said David Le Vine, deputy principal investigator at Goddard; he compared it to the glow of an electric stove. [https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/for-aquarius-sampling-seas-no-grain-of-salt-task/]
- "Other things being equal, salty water appears cooler to the radiometers than freshwater." [JPL science writers' guide]
- The instrument had to detect salinity changes of about 2 parts per 10,000, corresponding to roughly 0.1 K in brightness temperature. [JPL science writers' guide ; spacewar/JPL 2011 article]
- Open-ocean salinity ranges from about 32 to 37 parts per thousand. [JPL guide]
- Accuracy requirement: monthly maps with accuracy 0.2 practical salinity units at 150 km (93 mile) resolution; described by NASA as "a pinch of salt in a gallon of water". [JPL guide; phys.org 2011 https://phys.org/news/2011-06-nasa-surface-salinity.pdf]
- Three radiometers shared one 2.5 m (8.2 ft) antenna reflector and imaged a 390 km (242 mi) swath. [https://aquarius.oceansciences.org/cgi/data_measurement.htm]
- The radiometers sensed the top 10.1 mm (about 0.4 inch) of the ocean. [https://essic.umd.edu/nasas-salt-mapping-instrument-celebrates-first-year-in-orbit]
- Sampling every 10 microseconds, to help detect and mitigate radio frequency interference. L-band is reserved for radio astronomy, but out-of-band human-made signals, such as communications and air traffic control radar, can still overwhelm the natural signal. [https://aquarius.oceansciences.org/cgi/data_measurement.htm]
- A scatterometer with the same footprint transmits a microwave pulse and measures backscatter; rougher seas scatter more; this corrects for surface roughness, the largest source of error. [same page]
- Wentz: "It's basically a big subtraction process"; "Instead, because of waves, it's more like a funhouse mirror that distorts everything." Frank Wentz, Aquarius science team member, director of Remote Sensing Systems. [https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/for-aquarius-sampling-seas-no-grain-of-salt-task/]
- Le Vine: "It is a very tiny signal in a noisy environment." [JPL science writers' guide]
- 2003 PALS prototype flew on a C-130 aircraft; the results showed sea surface roughness dominated the natural salinity signal, so the instrument design added a scatterometer. [https://aquarius.oceansciences.org/cgi/ov_mission.htm]
- Orbit: sun-synchronous, seven-day repeat; the instrument gave global coverage every seven days. [JPL guide; data_measurement page]

Disputed or not to be used
- Date of the end: NASA says the mission ended 8 June 2015. Wikipedia and WMO OSCAR give the failure as 7 June 2015 (Wikipedia: telemetry 12:53:17 UTC, mission declared over 8 June, "recovery efforts failed"). Story uses NASA's 8 June and does not say anything about recovery attempts.
- Altitude today: trackers disagree (about 633 km versus about 645 km). Not used in the story. The 10 to 25 year orbital lifetime estimate comes from one tracker and is not used.
- Teaspoon fraction: JPL 2011 says one-eighth teaspoon per gallon; NASA's archived science page says about one sixth. Only "a pinch of salt in a gallon of water" is used.
- "More measurements in its first two months than the 125-year record from ships and buoys": the archived NASA page (via the Britannica summary) says first two months; PacIOOS (5 April 2013) phrases it as over the whole mission. Not used.
- Lead sentence of Wikipedia SAC-D says "power system failure" while the body says RTU failure. Story follows NASA wording (preliminary).
- Wikipedia's planned mission life: five years for SAC-D, three years for Aquarius primary mission. Not used.
- Spacecraft attitude after failure (tumbling etc.): not sourced, not used.
- Wikipedia's claim that Aquarius was "the first to combine" passive and active L-band is NASA's own wording ("first mission to combine passive and active measurements at L-band") and is true to NASA but not used.
- Cost: not found. Not used.
- Orbital Radar's "Active" label is a tracker label and must not be read as operating.
