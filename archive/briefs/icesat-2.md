# Brief: ICESat-2

Angle: One number examined from every side: twelve. A laser sends out trillions of photons and about a dozen come back, and that dozen is enough to measure ice. A mission built on how little returns.

Form: A single number examined from every side. Open on the dozen, explain the stopwatch, take the number through the beam design, the predecessor, the troubled build, the storm, the glacier, and the laser's lifespan, then close by returning to the dozen.

Weights: technology 40, history 25, reflection 20, culture 15.

Entry: "About twelve." The number of photons that come back to the telescope.

Technology to explain: Photon-counting lidar. A timer starts when a pulse leaves; a returning photon that passes a filter admitting only the laser's own green trips a detector and stops the timer; the travel time, with the satellite's position and the speed of light, gives the distance. Filter matters because sunlight contains the same colour. Software finds the surface where the stronger signals gather out of the scatter of dots. Six beams in three pairs give slope.

Culture (light): Counting a small total one coin at a time: a coat pocket tipped onto a table. Tangent only, no claim about the satellite.

Humour: Lowering the requirements when the laser would not meet them. A crystal cracked by a mounting that was too tight. The narrator would have given up by the third pulse.

Reflection: Nearly everything you send out does not come back, and the mission is built on that. The ice at one site went down, up, down again; there is no straight line.

Ending: Return to the dozen and to the narrator's admission: two trillion shots by March 2025, a dozen at a time.

Dark comedy dose: 3

Length: about 780 words.

## Fact sheet

Status
- NASA's mission page lists ICESat-2 as an "Active Mission"; the page was last modified 12 Aug 2026. [https://science.nasa.gov/mission/icesat-2/]
- WMO OSCAR listing: operational, last updated 18 December 2023, end of life "2026 or later". [https://tools.wmo.int/satellites/view/icesat_2]
- In March 2025 ATLAS instrument scientist Anthony Martino said the laser was "in excellent shape, even after six years and a couple trillion shots" and at the current rate could last well into the 2030s; a second laser aboard could be used if needed. [https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/icesat-2/2025/03/14/icesat-2s-laser-fires-2-trillionth-shot-spots-clouds/]
- ATLAS fired its 2 trillionth shot on 9 March 2025 (16:51 UTC). [same]
- Designed for three years, with propellant for seven. [https://www.nasa.gov/sites/default/files/atoms/files/factsheet082820180.pdf]

Launch
- Launched 15 September 2018 from Vandenberg, on the final Delta II. [https://science.nasa.gov/earth/10-things-to-know-about-icesat-2-nasas-latest-space-laser]

Instrument and photon counting
- ATLAS is the satellite's only scientific instrument. [10 things page]
- It fires 10,000 pulses per second; the first ICESat fired 40 per second. [10 things page; https://icesat-2.gsfc.nasa.gov/space-lasers]
- Laser is 532 nm, green. Filters admit only that wavelength so reflected sunlight does not swamp the detectors. [space-lasers page]
- About 300 trillion photons leave per pulse; only about a dozen return to the telescope. [space-lasers page; 10 things page]
- A timer starts when the pulse leaves; a photon passing the filters triggers a detector that stops it. Round trip takes about 3.3 milliseconds. [space-lasers page]
- Each round trip is timed to within 800 picoseconds, "nearly a billionth of a second". Engineers custom-built a stopwatch-like timing device because no existing timer met the requirements. [10 things page]
- Software builds "photon cloud" graphs of thousands of data points, with background photons from sunlight and particles or clouds in the air, and picks out the stronger signals to find surface elevation. [space-lasers page]
- Six beams in three pairs, which lets scientists estimate the slope of the surface. The first ICESat had one beam. [10 things page]
- Strong beam has four times the pulse energy of its paired weak beam; design target is 12 photoelectrons per shot in each strong beam for a return from clean snow under clear sky. [Martino et al., https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230010087/downloads/Martino_12512-17v2.pdf]
- Two lasers, a primary and a backup. [space-lasers page]
- Can detect annual elevation change in the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets as small as 4 millimetres. [NASA fact sheet PDF]

Predecessor
- Original ICESat: Laser 1 activated 20 Feb 2003, stopped unexpectedly on 29 March 2003 after about 36 days of data; measurements paused several months for an engineering review; resumed 25 Sept 2003 with Laser 2. [https://icesat.gsfc.nasa.gov/icesat/history.php]

Troubled build
- Spaceflight Now, 1 June 2014: a GAO report in April found the systems engineering team did an insufficient analysis on the laser sensor; engineers "found the laser altimeter's requirements were too stringent, and managers relaxed the sensor's operating constraints." NASA put a different team on the job. [https://spaceflightnow.com/news/n1406/01icesat2/]
- NASA, 16 Aug 2017: a 2016 Goddard test found a mounting device inside the laser was too tight, which fractured a crystal; the flight lasers were repaired and retested. [https://www.nasa.gov/feature/goddard/2017/nasas-icesat-2-preps-for-laser-tests/ (read via summary only; verbatim re-check failed)]

May 2024 solar storm
- Solar storms created unexpected drag; an automated response turned ATLAS off from 10 May; the spacecraft dropped about 6 km (3.7 miles) vertically. Thrusters restored the orbit, ATLAS was restarted, science mode resumed 21 June (UTC) 2024. No damage detected. [https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/icesat-2/2024/07/02/nasas-icesat-2-resumes-data-collection-after-solar-storms]
- The storm swelled parts of Earth's atmosphere, causing the drag. [https://scitechdaily.com/nasas-icesat-2-satellite-survives-strongest-solar-storm-in-20-years/]

Vanderford Glacier
- Described as East Antarctica's fastest-retreating glacier according to a recent study cited by NASA; between 2019 and 2022 the ice surface at one site dropped about six feet, rose a few feet the next year, then dropped again in 2024. [March 2025 NASA blog, above]

## Disputed or not to be used

- Photons per pulse: NASA says about 300 trillion; Wikipedia says about 20 trillion. Story says only "trillions".
- Altitude: about 500 km (NASA fact sheet), 496 km (Wikipedia text), 481 km (WMO). Not used.
- Orbit period: about 90 minutes (NASA 10 things) vs 94.22 minutes (Wikipedia). Not used.
- Mass and power: 1,580 kg / 1,374 W (NASA) vs 1,514 kg / 1,200 W (Wikipedia). Not used.
- Cost overrun and budget figures (2014 press, $559 million set 2012, about $200 million overrun) not used.
- ICESat-1 end date: 14 or 30 August 2010 in different parts of eoPortal. Not used.
- NASA mission page "last modified 12 Aug 2026" was read through a summarising fetch; it confirms listing as active, not a health check.
- No source found for "centimetre precision" as stated in the catalogue.
