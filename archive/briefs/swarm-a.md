# Brief: Swarm A

Angle: To measure an invisible field you must keep the instrument away from the machine that carries it. A satellite that is mostly a pole with its own magnetism designed out, flying in a trio, kept in the sky against a swollen atmosphere, measuring a north that will not stay put.

Form: A single object examined from every side: the four-metre boom. Start at its tip, walk back along it (tip instrument, halfway bench, body), then step off it into the trio, the debris warning and the field itself, and return to the tip at the end.

Weights: technology 40, history 25, culture 15, reflection 20.

Entry: The end of the boom, four metres from the body, where the scalar magnetometer sits.

Technology to explain: Why a boom. Electronics carry current and current makes its own magnetism, so the instruments sit as far from the body as possible. A scalar magnetometer at the tip (optically pumped helium) measures only the strength of the field. A vector magnetometer halfway along measures its direction and is calibrated against the tip instrument; star trackers beside it say which way the instrument is pointing. One exact image: a ruler checking another ruler, with a compass that must know which way it faces.

Culture: A phone compass app that points with confidence in a car park (tangent, no satellite claim). Maps of the field are reissued at least every five years because north will not hold still.

Humour: The boom as a spacecraft that tries to be as absent as possible. Reaction wheels left out for their magnetism. Launch schedule that moved faster than the field. Debris with eight hours' notice and no identity. The narrator cannot detect a nanotesla.

Reflection: We measure something we cannot feel, and it is changing, and the proper response is a new edition of the map. Be plain about what is not known (current status of Alpha alone).

Ending: Return to the tip of the boom: an instrument that knows what it knows by staying away from itself.

Dark comedy dose: 3

Length: about 830 words.

## Fact sheet

Identity and trio
- Swarm is ESA's first Earth Observation constellation: three identical satellites, Alpha, Bravo and Charlie, launched 22 November 2013 into a near-polar orbit; full constellation achieved 17 April 2014. [earth.esa.int/eogateway/missions/swarm]
- Swarm A is Alpha. A and C form the lower pair flying side by side; B flies higher. Initial altitudes 462 km (A, C) and 511 km (B); inclination 87.35 degrees (A, C), 87.75 degrees (B). [earth.esa.int/eogateway/missions/swarm; Swarm description page]
- Launch from Plesetsk on a Rockot with a Briz-KM upper stage, all three on one rocket. [spaceref.com; russianspaceweb.com]
- ESA says the satellites sit as close as 8 cm apart on the upper stage. [ESA Ralf Bock profile page]
- In 2018 the e-POP instrument from Canada's CASSIOPE satellite was added as a fourth element, called Swarm Echo (Swarm-E). [earth.esa.int/eogateway/news/swarm-set-for-continued-success-after-10-years-in-orbit; mission overview page]
- Primary objectives include core dynamics, lithospheric magnetisation, mantle conductivity, and ionosphere and magnetosphere currents. [ESA mission overview page]

Boom and instruments
- Each boom is four metres long; deployed, each satellite is just over nine metres. Boom folds for launch, deploys in orbit, and swings gently before locking. [ESA, "Swarm constellation deploys booms", 23 Nov 2013]
- Scalar magnetometer (ASM) at the end of the boom, away from the body's electrical units. Vector field magnetometer and three star trackers on an optical bench halfway along. [same]
- ASM is an optically pumped helium-4 magnetometer that calibrates the vector instrument. [ESA Bulletin 151 facts and figures PDF, Aug 2012]
- Nearly every component was magnetically tested before installation; ESA says "Magnetic cleanliness is vital." [ESA, Ralf Bock system engineer page]
- Reaction wheels were rejected because of magnetic and vibration effects; attitude uses magnetorquers and cold-gas thrusters. [ESA Bulletin 151 PDF]

History
- ESA selected Swarm unanimously in a press release of 3 June 2004, with launch then planned for 2009. [ESA PR 30-2004 PDF at ftp.space.dtu.dk]
- Launch actually took place 22 November 2013. Russianspaceweb lists several postponements. [russianspaceweb.com/rockot_swarm.html]
- Planned mission life was four years after commissioning; extended to 2021, then 2025. [ESA mission overview page]

Debris and orbit
- Debris spotted heading for Alpha at 16:00 CEST on 30 June 2022, collision predicted about eight hours later, shortly after midnight. Normally warning is more than 24 hours. Teams planned and carried out the manoeuvre in about four hours. Debris identity not given. [ESA, "Swarm dodges collision during climb to escape Sun's wrath", 14 July 2022]
- Alpha and Charlie needed 25 manoeuvres over 10 weeks to reach higher orbits. [same]
- Without raises, A and C would have re-entered in 2024, because solar cycle 25 expands the atmosphere. [earth.esa.int/eogateway/missions/swarm/constellation-and-orbit-evolution]

The field
- Over the last 200 years the field has lost about 9 per cent of its strength on a global average. [ESA, "Swarm probes weakening of Earth's magnetic field"]
- South Atlantic Anomaly minimum fell from about 24,000 nanotesla (1970) to about 22,000 (2020). [same]
- Sir James Clark Ross found the magnetic north pole in northern Canada in 1831. [ScienceAlert, 23 Jan 2025, quoting BGS modeller]
- Magnetic north moved about 10 km a year from 1600 to 1990, sped up in the 2000s, and slowed to about 35 km a year over the past five years, still heading toward Siberia. [BGS, 1 Jan 2025]
- BGS geophysicist Ciaran Beggan: "The current behaviour of magnetic north is something that we have never observed before." [BGS, 1 Jan 2025]
- NOAA's World Magnetic Model is used by smartphone makers for compass apps; the model is updated at least every five years because the field changes unpredictably. [NOAA NCEI, 17 Dec 2024]
- Weak-field area of the South Atlantic Anomaly has grown by roughly half the area of mainland Europe since 2014. [ESA, 13 Oct 2025]

Status (dated)
- ESA article of 13 October 2025: Swarm has 11 years of data; the team plans to extend the record beyond 2030. [ESA, "Swarm reveals growing weak spot..."]
- EGU 2026 abstract, updated 14 March 2026: more than 12 years in orbit and the constellation "continues to deliver high-quality measurements". [meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-10534.html]
- No source found that speaks of Alpha alone after 2023. Status of Alpha in particular is not confirmed.

## Disputed or not to be used

- Field loss: ESA says about 9 per cent over 200 years; NASA Earth Observatory says about 5 per cent over the past century. Story attributes the ESA figure to ESA.
- Spacing on upper stage (8 cm): the same page says the satellites are about four metres long on the stage, another ESA source says 5.04 m stowed. Attributed in the story, low importance.
- Initial altitudes differ by source (450, 460, 462, 490 km at injection); the story uses none. Lower pair altitude later: about 440 km (2021) per ESA, rose by about 45 km in 2022.
- Pole speed: BGS says peak up to 55 km a year, ScienceAlert says about 50. Story uses only the 10 and 35 figures.
- Do not use: lower pair re-entry outlook (2027, 2028, 2034) from a conference slide deck, uncertain and not about Alpha alone. Do not use mission cost (not found), debris identity, the reason for the side-by-side pair (not stated in the sources fetched), or any current altitude of Alpha (not found).
- Catalogue NORAD number 39452 for Swarm A could not be checked (CelesTrak blocked). Wikipedia and Swarm fetches were summaries only.
