# Brief: Vanguard 1

Angle: A promise of two thousand years, made about a ball the size of a grapefruit, and what happened each time someone checked it. The failures before it, the dented-bowl orbit that showed the Earth's shape, and the little machine that outlived every satellite before it and every explanation for its silence.

Form: A promise and its audit. Open on the original lifetime estimate (about 2,000 years), run through the two failed attempts and the success, explain what the orbit taught, and close on the estimate being revised down to about 240 years, with the satellite going round regardless.

Weights: history 40, technology 25, culture 15, reflection 20.

Entry: The lifetime estimate given at launch: about two thousand years for the ball and the casing of the last stage.

Technology to explain: Geodesy from orbit. A satellite in an elliptical orbit follows a path set by gravity; if the Earth were perfectly symmetrical the lowest point of the orbit would stay at one height, and Vanguard 1's perigee height varied, which showed the polar profile of the geoid is not symmetrical about the equator. Image: a marble in a bowl, and a dent in the bowl. Also the two power sources: batteries for about twenty days, six solar cells for years.

Culture: "Grapefruit satellite" (attributed to Khrushchev, cited to a US Navy source) and the Sputnik-pun press names for the failed first attempt (Flopnik, Kaputnik).

Humour: The satellite that was thrown clear of the exploding rocket and kept beeping; holding a launch for satellite traffic; the household machine nobody dares touch (tangent); estimates nobody will be around to be wrong about.

Reflection: Large forecasts shrink when someone checks them. The one with a joke for a nickname outlasted the ones taken seriously. The narrator keeps his own forecasts to himself.

Ending: "The ball goes round. It has no idea it has been revised."

Dark comedy dose: 3 (several dark turns; admissions: collecting uncheckable numbers, laughing at the newspaper puns twice, keeping own forecasts private; one tangent, the old machine in the house nobody dares touch; callback: the number that cannot be checked, returned to as the number that was checked and got shorter)

Length: about 800 words.

## Fact sheet

Status and orbit
- Vanguard 1 (NORAD 5, COSPAR 1958-002B) is described as still in orbit, now derelict, the oldest artificial object in orbit together with its launch vehicle's upper stage. [Wikipedia, Vanguard 1; Orbital Radar page, last updated 13 September 2026]
- Orbital elements at epoch 1 August 2026: perigee 661 km, apogee 3,824 km, inclination 34.24 degrees, period 132.59 min. [KeepTrack, keeptrack.space/satellite/5]
- On the night of 25 to 26 April 2026 the 40.2 cm MISO telescope at TU Delft imaged both Vanguard 1 and its upper stage. [sattrackcam blog, 26 April 2026; Wikipedia]
- No transmissions since May 1964; it has been tracked optically since. [NRL via Wikipedia; Smithsonian Air & Space; steeljawscribe]
- Vanguard 1 was the fourth artificial satellite successfully orbited, after Sputnik 1, Sputnik 2 and Explorer 1; by 2018 none of those three was still in orbit. [Engadget, 16 March 2018; Wikipedia]

Size and hardware
- Aluminium sphere, about 6 inches across (sources give 6 in, 6.4 in, 15 cm, 16.3 cm, 16.5 cm), mass about 1.46 to 1.47 kg. [Wikipedia; NRL; Space.com; Orbital Radar; NASA SP-4202 chap. 12 says 6.4 in and 3.5 lb]
- Two transmitters: 10 mW at 108 MHz on mercury batteries, 5 mW at 108.03 MHz on six solar cells mounted on the body. [Wikipedia; steeljawscribe]
- Battery transmitter: temperature data about 16 days, tracking about 20 days. [Wikipedia; Engadget: batteries lasted about 20 days]
- First satellite powered by solar cells. The solar transmitter worked until May 1964, last signals at the Minitrack station in Quito, Ecuador. [Wikipedia; NRL; steeljawscribe]
- Space.com (4 April 2025): Matt Bille (Booz Allen) said the silence in 1964 was because solar output fell below the power the transmitter needed; whether the satellite is intact, spinning or tumbling is unknown. [Space.com]

Failures and launch
- TV-3, 6 December 1957, Cape Canaveral: rose about four feet (some sources about one metre), lost thrust, fell back and exploded. The satellite was thrown clear, landed apart, still transmitting; too damaged to reuse. [NASA SP-4202 chap. 11; NASA 60-years-ago article; Wikipedia, Vanguard TV-3]
- Press nicknames: Flopnik, Kaputnik, among others. [NASA article; Wikipedia]
- TV-3BU, 5 February 1958: control failure, vehicle broke up at about 55 to 57 seconds. [Wikipedia, Project Vanguard; NASA SP-4202 appendix]
- Vanguard 1 launched 17 March 1958 (TV-4). Countdown had a "stretch-out" hold so that Explorer I's pass overhead would not interfere with the payload's signals. Liftoff 7:15:41 a.m. local. [NASA SP-4202 chap. 12]
- John Hagen directed the project from Washington; after a San Diego Minitrack station confirmed both frequencies (108.03 and 108.00) he phoned Alan Waterman, Director of the National Science Foundation: "It is in orbit. You can inform the President." [NASA SP-4202 chap. 12]
- Khrushchev described it as "the grapefruit satellite" (cited by Wikipedia to a US Navy Spacecraft Engineering Department source). [Wikipedia, Project Vanguard]

What the orbit taught
- Variations in Vanguard 1's perigee height showed the polar section of the geoid is not symmetrical about the equator: a slight pear shape with the stem at the North Pole; sea level at the North Pole about 40 m farther from the equator than at the South Pole (King-Hele and Cook, Nature, 9 November 1973). [Nature 246, 86 abstract]
- The original analysis: O'Keefe, Eckels and Squires, "Vanguard Measurements Give Pear-Shaped Component of Earth's Figure", Science, 1959. [NASA NTRS record 19660028058]
- NRL: its orbital data revealed the Earth is slightly pear-shaped rather than round. [NRL, navy.mil; Engadget]

Lifetime
- At launch the expected lifetime of the satellite and its trailing third-stage casing was about 2,000 years. [NASA SP-4202 chap. 12]
- Solar radiation pressure and atmospheric drag during high solar activity reduced it to about 240 years; Wikipedia gives an estimated decay date of 2198. [Wikipedia; steeljawscribe; Orbital Radar]
- Smithsonian Air & Space says it will remain in orbit "until at least 2109". [Smithsonian Air & Space]

Retrieval idea
- April 2025: Matt Bille's team proposed imaging then possibly retrieving it; nothing funded or scheduled; no cost figure. [Space.com, 4 April 2025]

## Disputed or not to be used

- Catalogue says "will remain for 600 more years". Not supported. Sources give about 240 years in total (decay about 2198), or at least 2109. Do not use 600.
- Catalogue "oldest human-made object still in orbit": true only together with its upper stage, which is the same age. Story avoids the superlative.
- Diameter and mass vary by source (15 to 16.5 cm; 1.4 to 1.47 kg): story says "about six inches" and "about one and a half kilograms".
- Orbit period and perigee/apogee differ between launch, mid-life and current figures; story gives none.
- Pole difference: 40 m (King-Hele and Cook 1973 abstract) versus 45 m (later refinements, CSMonitor 1980, Wikiwand). Story attributes about 40 m to a later paper.
- Decay year: 2198 (Wikipedia) versus "at least 2109" (Smithsonian). Story gives no year.
- Mass of 6.4 in, 4 lb in NASA text versus 1.46 kg elsewhere: avoid pounds.
- Solar cell size (about 5 cm on a side) came only from steeljawscribe: not used.
- Press name coiners (kaputnik by Daily Express etc.) came from a Wikipedia mirror: not used.
- Whiteeagleaerospace says "240 more years": wrong reading, not used.
- Unfetchable or blocked: nasa.gov history chapter 14 and history.nasa.gov, NSSDC page, direct Wikipedia first try (later fetched).
