# Brief: ISS

Angle: The most famous object in orbit, seen through one door: the hatch to a leaking tunnel that two agencies agreed to close in the evening. A station built by partners who each look after their own part, and the unfinished question of who looks after the whole, up to and including its end.

Form: One door. Open on the evening compromise, widen to the tunnel, the treaty and the money, and come back to the same door at the close.

Weights: history 25, technology 25, culture 25, reflection 25.

Entry: Autumn 2024, a NASA official describing the compromise: the hatch is closed in the evening.

Technology to explain: The station as a sealed can of air falling around the Earth at about five miles a second, one lap roughly every ninety minutes, kept up by occasional reboosts against a trace of atmosphere, so that the whole point of the place is that the air stays where it is put. Then what a crack in one tunnel means for that.

Culture: The shared-flat arrangement (Article 5, jurisdiction by registered element; Zarya funded by one partner and built by another); housekeeping done by the crew themselves (vents, bathroom, kitchen, lost pencils); the plan for the end and who does what in it.

Humour: The compromise being a door and a time of day. The two agencies not agreeing what leak rate counts as untenable. Narrator admits relief at a small number. Tangent: the dead hallway bulb in a shared flat that nobody wants to own, returned from with "The station has a hallway like that."

Reflection: The part of a partnership nobody photographs is the agreement about the ending. One paragraph, alone.

Ending: The latest leak figures found are from 2024, so say so. Occupied since November 2000; Crew-13 through the hatch on 1 October 2026. If the compromise still holds, a door is being closed this evening. Quiet.

Dark comedy dose: 3 (several dark turns; admissions: relief at 3.7 pounds, recognising the lost pencils; one tangent; the "evening" door as the returning line)

Length: about 780 words.

## Fact sheet

Status
- The station was "continually occupied since November 2000" [NASA, https://www.nasa.gov/?p=419068; NASA fact sheet April 2020, https://www.discoverspace.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/NASA-ISS-Diagram-and-Facts-4.2020.pdf].
- On 1 October 2026 the hatch opened and the Crew-13 members (Jessica Watkins, Luke Delaney, Joshua Kutryk, Sergey Teteryatnikov) entered the station and joined Expedition 75 [NASA station blog, 1 Oct 2026, https://nasa.gov/blogs/spacestation/2026/10/01/crew-13-members-enter-station-join-expedition-75]. This is the dated source for "occupied".
- Speed about 5 miles per second, one orbit roughly every 90 minutes [NASA fact sheet April 2020]. Altitude about 415 km (260 miles) in mid-2024 [SpacePolicyOnline, 26 June 2024]. The catalogue says "400km above": approximate only.
- Mass about 430 metric tons (965,000 lb) [SpacePolicyOnline, https://spacepolicyonline.com/news/nasa-picks-spacex-to-build-iss-deorbit-vehicle/]. NASA fact sheet 2020 says 419,725 kg; use "roughly".
- The ISS needs periodic reboosts to counter atmospheric drag [SpacePolicyOnline, same article].

Origin and law
- Zarya launched 20 November 1998 from Baikonur on a Proton rocket; the first component of the station [NASA, https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/20-years-ago-construction-began-international-space-station/; https://www.nasa.gov/international-space-station/zarya-module/].
- Zarya: "U.S.-funded and Russian-built", built by Khrunichev in Moscow under a subcontract to Boeing for NASA; "a U.S. component of the station, although it was built and launched by Russia"; orbited alone 16 days before Endeavour arrived [NASA Zarya page].
- Intergovernmental Agreement signed 29 January 1998. Article 5: "each partner shall retain jurisdiction and control over the elements it registers" [ESA legal framework page, https://www.esa.int/Our_Activities/Human_Spaceflight/International_Space_Station/International_Space_Station_legal_framework].

The leak
- The leak is in the PrK, a tunnel connecting Zvezda to a docking port used by Progress cargo ships; Zvezda is the Russian module and launched in 2000 [SpacePolicyOnline, 13 Nov 2024, https://spacepolicyonline.com/news/u-s-russia-disagree-on-causes-risks-of-leaks-on-iss/; Space.com, 16 Oct 2024, https://space.com/iss-leak-50-areas-of-concern-nasa-report].
- First leak reported as September 2019 [SpacePolicyOnline, citing NASA OIG Sept 2024; Space.com says "since 2019"]. See disputed.
- NASA and Roscosmos identified 50 "areas of concern" and were tracking four cracks. Roscosmos covered all four with "a combination of sealant and patches" [Space.com, 16 Oct 2024].
- Leak rose to 2.4 pounds per day (from a historic low of 0.2) and peaked at 3.7 pounds per day in 2024; repairs cut the high rate by roughly a third [Space.com, 16 Oct 2024; https://space.com/iss-leak-safety-risk-nasa-oig-report]. Month of the peak is inconsistent between sources: do not give it.
- Rated 5 on a 5-point scale in NASA's risk system as of August 2024 [NASA OIG IG-24-020, https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/ig-24-020.pdf, p. 12 fn. 27].
- NASA and Roscosmos "continuously monitor leak rates and close the hatch to the Service Module when access is not required"; they had not agreed on the leak rate at which it becomes untenable; keeping the hatch permanently closed removes one cargo delivery port and requires additional propellant to maintain altitude and attitude [OIG IG-24-020, p. 12].
- After May and June 2024 meetings with Russian counterparts, NASA raised the issue to the highest level of its risk system. The Joint Commission met in Moscow on 17-18 September 2024 and could not agree on causes or risks [SpacePolicyOnline, 13 Nov 2024].
- Roscosmos: cyclic fatigue from micro-vibrations the most probable cause. NASA: multiple causes [SpacePolicyOnline, 13 Nov 2024].
- NASA associate administrator Jim Free: "We've come to a compromise that they close it in the evening" (as quoted by Space.com, 16 Oct 2024). The story paraphrases.
- The latest leak figures found are from autumn 2024. Current state of the leak unknown to me.

Money and upkeep
- The ISS costs about $3 billion a year, roughly a third of NASA's annual human spaceflight budget [NASA OIG IG-22-005, https://oig.nasa.gov/docs/IG-22-005.pdf].
- The ISS "never returns to Earth", so crew must maintain it in orbit; crew vacuum vents and panels, disinfect equipment, clean bathroom and kitchen areas; astronauts "often find lost pencils and tools in the air vents" [Canadian Space Agency, https://asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/astronauts/about-the-job/maintenance-of-the-iss.asp].

The end
- NASA awarded SpaceX an $843 million contract on 26 June 2024 to build the US Deorbit Vehicle (launch excluded). NASA will own and operate it. Planned around 2030, re-entry over an unpopulated part of the South Pacific near Point Nemo [SpacePolicyOnline, 26 June 2024].
- Russian Progress vehicles were the original plan for deorbit; Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine changed that [SpacePolicyOnline, 26 June 2024].
- 6 August 2026: a Russian outlet (www1.ru, citing RIAN) quoted a Roscosmos representative saying the deorbit would be in 2028-2030, with two Russian Progress craft and one US vehicle carrying it out jointly, and that Roscosmos and NASA still plan to agree how to divide responsibilities [https://www1.ru/en/news/2026/08/06/426733-rossiia-i-ssa-svedut-mks-s-orbity-v-2028-2030-godax.amp.html]. Secondary only.

## Disputed or not to be used
- Catalogue "400km above": about 415 km in 2024 per SpacePolicyOnline. Do not state an altitude.
- Date of first leak: SpacePolicyOnline says September 2019 citing the OIG report; the OIG report text read for this brief did not state it (it cites its 2022 report for earlier coverage). The story says "since 2019", which both news sources support.
- Month of the 3.7 lb/day peak (April per Space.com; OIG gives both February and April). Not used.
- Deorbit date: "around 2030" (NASA, 2024) versus "2028-2030" (Roscosmos via Russian outlet, 2026). Story says around 2030 for the plan and reports the August item as a quotation by a Russian outlet.
- Number of people aboard now: NASA blog lists 11 names but may be incomplete. Not used.
- "Most expensive object ever built" and similar superlatives: not sourced, not used.
- The NASA site deorbit page and some NASA URLs were not fetchable (not on allowlist); figures came from SpacePolicyOnline and the OIG instead.
