# Brief: Hubble Space Telescope

Angle: A flaw of 1.3 millimetres in a test device, passed over by a process that trusted its own most precise instrument, and a telescope that was not blind but badly short-sighted, then fitted with glasses.

Form: A single number examined from every side. Open on 1.3 millimetres, follow where it sits (a lens in a test rig), what it did to the light, how it got past two warnings, and how it was cancelled. Return to the glasses.

Weights: history 45, technology 25, culture 15, reflection 15.

Entry: The number 1.3 millimetres, and the tolerance it broke.

Technology to explain: What a primary mirror is for (bring every ray from a star to one point), what "too flat at the edge" does to that (edge rays miss the meeting point, so a star becomes a dot in fuzzy rings), and how the fix cancels the error afterwards with corrective optics rather than touching the mirror.

Culture: The blurry years were not wasted; the project scientist recalls about 40 per cent of projects went ahead, with image-restoration software. The corrected images "like putting my glasses on". COSTAR now in a museum.

Humour: The spacers that signalled trouble; the optician's "better, one or two?" tangent; trust in the most precise instrument; a second opinion only welcome from someone who agrees.

Reflection: Process trusts its best instrument. State plainly, give it a line alone.

Ending: The glasses are in a museum. They did their job. Hubble is still circling, with open questions about reboost.

Dark comedy dose: 3

Length: about 800 words.

## Fact sheet

The error
- The flaw was announced 27 June 1990, about two months after launch on 24 April 1990. The primary mirror was "too flat near its outer edge", ten times the specified tolerance. [NASA, science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/overview/hubbles-mirror-flaw]
- Primary mirror is 2.4 m (7.9 ft) across. [NASA mirror-flaw page]
- Cause: the reflective null corrector, used as the template for polishing, had its field lens about 1.3 mm too far from the lower mirror. [Allen Report, NTRS 19910003124; HST failure report pp. 6-9]
- Spacings had to be right to 10 micrometres; the error was 1,300 micrometres, 130 times the tolerance. [HST failure report p. 4-2]
- Error in the mirror: 0.4 wave rms at 632.8 nm, "ten times larger than the specified tolerance". [HST failure report p. iii]
- Mirror too flat by about 1/50 the width of a human hair. [Astronomy.com, "Techno-turkey", 2020; also NASA SM1 anniversary page]
- Historian Joseph Tatarewicz: the mirror was ground "nearly perfectly to the wrong specifications". [Physics Today, Sarah Scoles, 1 April 2020]
- Lens position was set by reflecting light off a metal rod end. The report says the operator appears to have got reflection from the field cap where the non-reflecting material had broken away, not from the rod end. [HST failure report p. 7-7; Hackaday 29 April 2020 quoting the report]
- The adjustment screws had no travel left, so spacers were put between the field lens and its mounting plate. [HST failure report p. 7-7]
- Polishing finished April 1981. [HST failure report p. 1-1] The device was found in the same configuration nine years earlier. [NASA mirror-flaw page]
- Two other test devices (inverse null corrector, refractive null corrector) showed the error; Perkin-Elmer discounted them as less reliable. The Board found them accurate. [HST failure report pp. 9-1, 9-2, 10-2; NTRS abstract]
- "There was great concern about cost and schedule, which further inhibited consideration of independent tests." No independent tests were planned. [Allen Report, NTRS abstract]
- Hubble failed a focusing test on 24 June 1990; images had fuzzy rings; testers diagnosed spherical aberration. [Astronomy.com 2020]

The blurry years
- Project scientist Robert O'Dell recalled about 40 per cent of projects went ahead. Sandra Faber's team wrote deconvolution algorithms. [Physics Today 2020]

The fix
- Servicing Mission 1, Endeavour, launched 2 December 1993. Five spacewalks totalling over 35 hours. Installed WFPC2 and COSTAR. COSTAR is refrigerator-sized with a set of mirrors. [NASA, science.nasa.gov/missions/hubble/hubble-celebrates-30th-anniversary-of-servicing-mission-1]
- WFPC2: its last mirror before the CCD was shaped to cancel the primary's error. COSTAR box held 10 small mirrors. Ed Weiler recalled Sandy Faber, after seeing corrected images: "my God, it's like putting my glasses on!" [CBS News, "An ingenious fix for Hubble's famously flawed vision"]
- First corrected star image came at 1 a.m. on 18 December 1993, viewed at the Space Telescope Science Institute, Baltimore. [NASA SM1 page]
- COSTAR removed May 2009 (SM4) for the Cosmic Origins Spectrograph; now at the Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum. [NASA mirror-flaw page]

Current status (checked 31 October 2026)
- Hubble completed its 200,000th orbit on 19 September 2026. [NASA, nasa.gov/image-article/nasas-hubble-telescope-reaches-milestone-looks-for-elusive-supernova, 23 Sept 2026]
- Space.com, 25 Sept 2026: still operating, about 483 km up; adapted to one working gyroscope in 2024; no further servicing planned. No more current gyro status found.
- 4 June 2024: NASA moved to one-gyro mode, second held in reserve; Mark Clampin: "we are not going to pursue a reboost right now". [Space.com, Mike Wall, 4 June 2024; SpacePolicyOnline 4 June 2024]
- 6 June 2026: NASA open to a reboost "provided its operating costs can be reduced"; no plan or decision. [SpaceNews, Jeff Foust, via lede]

## Disputed or not to be used

- Cause detail: the Allen Report says reflection from the field cap where non-reflecting material was missing. Other writers say black paint scraped or a "worn spot on a black-anodized cap". Story keeps to "non-reflecting coating".
- Error size in microns: 2 microns (NASA timeline), 2.2 microns (Physics Today). Not used; one-fiftieth of a hair used.
- Whether the technician noticed the gap and failed to report it, and any motive: speculation. Not used.
- "Washer" versus "spacers", and 3 mm washer (CBS): not used.
- Gyro counts disagree between sources (two or three working; 8 or 9 of 22 failed). Not used.
- Reentry predictions (McDowell 2028; STScI median mid-2033; NASA "mid-2030s") disagree. Not used beyond silence.
- Current gyro mode after 2024 not confirmed by a dated source; story says only what 2024 sources say.
- Kodak-Itek bid and the $64.2 million figure: from one secondary source. Not used.
- The Faber quote is Weiler's recollection, so story says "remembered".
