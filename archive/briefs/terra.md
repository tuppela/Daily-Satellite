# Brief: Terra

Angle: A satellite given one appointment, half past ten in the morning over the equator, and the quiet, years-long business of keeping it, then slowly losing it, because the fuel was being saved for the end.

Form: A single number examined from every side. The crossing time (10:30, then 10:15, then forecast 9:00) is the thread: why it matters, what it cost to hold, what happened when they stopped holding it, and the way it now keeps arriving earlier.

Weights: history 35, technology 25, culture 15, reflection 25.

Entry: The time itself, stated flat: half past ten in the morning. "That was the appointment."

Technology to explain: Why a fixed local hour matters (same sun angle, so the only change in the picture is the planet; earlier crossing means lower sun and more shadow). How MODIS builds a picture: a continuously turning two-sided scan mirror sweeps the view 55 degrees either side of straight down, each sweep reads a strip about 2,330 km wide, the satellite's motion moves the next sweep along, 36 bands of light including infrared, whole globe in one to two days.

Culture: Light. The nearly 100 data products and 27,000+ papers can be a passing mention at most. The team watching the spacecraft's health day and, until recently, night.

Humour: The fuel is being saved for the satellite's own disposal. Thanks as a rationed resource. The reliable colleague nobody has ever thanked (tangent). Data from the anomaly day marked "questionable or bad".

Reflection: We do not thank the thing that works; we notice it when it stops.

Ending: Quiet. NASA's projection of end of science in February 2027, "not an immediate ending". The narrator says thank you early, which is the drift in miniature.

Dark comedy dose: 3 (several dark turns; admissions: "I have made no such arrangements myself", "mostly I feel judged", "I'd get the same label"; one tangent, the person in every building who does the same job and is never thanked; callback: half past ten, then "a long way behind it now"; fuel returns as thanks rationed like fuel).

Length: about 725 words.

## Fact sheet

Mission and status
- Launched 18 December 1999 from Vandenberg; NASA's first Earth Observing System flagship mission; planned as a six-year mission and has outlasted that by nearly two decades. [NASA, "Terra: The End of an Era", published 29 Dec 2025, updated 20 Mar 2026, https://science.nasa.gov/science-research/earth-science/terra-the-end-of-an-era]
- Completed more than 100,000 orbits; data cited in more than 27,000 scientific publications "as of this writing" (Dec 2025). [same NASA article]
- Five instruments: ASTER, CERES, MISR, MODIS, MOPITT. [https://terra.nasa.gov/, page marked last updated 16 Oct 2026; fetched 30 Oct 2026]
- Editor's note: end-of-mission language "not intended to suggest an immediate conclusion"; end-of-science projected "due to fuel and power limitations" for Feb 2027 (Terra). [NASA article above]
- Status evidence, dated: MODIS entered standby after a Terra anomaly about 20:00 UTC on 26 March 2026 and returned to science mode about 19:23 UTC on 27 March 2026; the notice gives no cause. Data from the gap were flagged questionable or bad; data after were "safe to use". [NASA Earthdata alert "Terra MODIS Placed in Standby Mode", last updated 31 Mar 2026, https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/alerts-outages/terra-modis-placed-standby-mode]
- terra.nasa.gov (last updated 16 Oct 2026) says Terra has been drifting to an earlier equator crossing time since 2020 and lists one CERES, MISR and MODIS as still operating. The tool summary of that page flagged internal inconsistency (see disputed). It is not a retired or decayed satellite as of these sources; no source found says it has been decommissioned. [https://terra.nasa.gov/]

Orbit and the appointment
- Equator crossing time held at 10:30 am (10:45 am for the first two years) by inclination adjust manoeuvres, about four times a year on average. [NASA Terra, "Terra Begins to Drift in Time", 3 Aug 2020, https://terra.nasa.gov/?p=190003; also terra.nasa.gov project scientist message]
- Last inclination adjust manoeuvre: 27 February 2020. After it, Terra began drifting to an earlier crossing time. [same NASA 2020 post]
- NASA says the decision to stop the adjustments was to preserve fuel "to conserve Terra's remaining fuel to allow for controlled reentry". [NASA article, Dec 2025]
- By fall 2022 the crossing time had shifted from 10:30 to 10:15, and the orbit had dropped about 5 km. [NASA article, Dec 2025]
- A 2020 forecast (explicitly "subject to change") predicted 9:00 am crossing by December 2025. [NASA 2020 post]
- Earlier crossing: lower solar elevation, more shadows, lower cloud probability, lower surface temperatures, weaker reflected signal. [NASA project scientist message, https://terra.nasa.gov/about/terras-orbit-changes/terra-orbital-drift-information/a-message-from-the-project-scientist]
- Mission operators monitored the satellite's energy and performance day and, until quite recently, night. [NASA article, Dec 2025]

Instruments and decline
- MODIS: continuously rotating double-sided scan mirror sweeping +/-55 degrees; mirror drive designed for 100 per cent duty cycle over a six-year design life; 36 spectral bands from 0.4 to 14.4 micrometres; at the EOS orbit of 705 km gives a 2,330 km swath and global coverage every one to two days. [https://modis.gsfc.nasa.gov/about/design.php; JPL: https://science.jpl.nasa.gov/projects/modis/ for swath, day-or-two revisit and 36 bands]
- Battery degradation has forced power reductions. MOPITT (carbon monoxide) safe mode in early Feb 2025 (NASA gives 1 Feb in one place, 4 Feb in another), turned off 9 April 2025 to save power for the other instruments. [NASA article; terra.nasa.gov]
- One of two CERES instruments is no longer functional; ASTER's SWIR subsystem is no longer operational, with limitations to ASTER's telescopes. [NASA article, Dec 2025]

Naming (not used in story)
- Sasha Jones won a NASA and American Geophysical Union essay contest to name the satellite; prize included equipment for Brentwood High School, Missouri. [ScienceDaily, 24 Sep 1999, https://sciencedaily.com/releases/1999/09/990923160941.htm]

## Disputed or not to be used

- Catalogue description "NASA's flagship Earth observer. Watching the planet change since 1999." Broadly fine: NASA calls Terra the EOS flagship; launched Dec 1999 (data collection began 24 Feb 2000 per Wikipedia). It omits that Terra is now near the end of its science life and drifting off its original orbit; not wrong.
- Crossing time now: terra.nasa.gov page gives 9:06 MLT in a caption and 9:59 in a chart alt text. Story gives only the 10:15 (fall 2022) figure and the 2020 forecast.
- Timing of the decision to stop the manoeuvres: last manoeuvre 27 Feb 2020 (NASA 2020 post), but the NASA Dec 2025 article says "late 2020". Story gives only the last-manoeuvre date.
- Passivation/decommission date: none stated by NASA; do not give one. Wikipedia infobox shows "Decommissioned" and "last contact Q1 2026 (planned)", contradicted by NASA sources above. Not used.
- Wikipedia's "Latin word for Earth" vs the 1999 press story's "Greek name for Earth". Not used.
- 2008 hacking of Terra's command systems (Wikipedia and US-China Commission reporting, via media): attribution is contested and NBC page could not be fetched. Not used.
- 2009 battery cell failure "likely micrometeoroid" and the 12 to 14 motor-drive false shutdowns a month: Wikipedia only. Not used.
- Launch vehicle (Atlas IIAS), launch mass, period: Wikipedia only. Not used.
- Terra's 705 km altitude and 98-minute period: story does not state them.
