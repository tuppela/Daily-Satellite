# Brief: ICEYE X2

Angle: A radar the industry said could not fit in a small satellite, built by two friends from a university course, and the way a company declares a mission finished while the machine carries on. Finnish pride, said as quietly as possible.

Form: A relay in two parts. Open on the first satellite, ICEYE-X1, and its ten seconds over Alaska, because that is where the proof came from. Hand the story across to ICEYE-X2, the one that is still in orbit, and let it end there. The two machines share one entry; X2 is the one on the globe.

Weights: history 40, technology 25, culture 20, reflection 15.

Entry: 21:47 UTC, Monday 15 January 2018. A satellite over Alaska looks down for about ten seconds.

Technology to explain: How a radar this small can make a sharp picture. Radar sends its own radio waves and listens for the echo, so it needs neither sunlight nor a clear sky. A small antenna alone would give a blurry image. The trick is the satellite's own movement: as it travels it listens to the same patch of ground from many slightly different positions, and software combines all those echoes as though one very large antenna had listened at once. One exact physical image: a long row of small photographs taken from a moving train and stitched into one picture that no single window could have given.

Culture: Light and dry. The name is ice plus eye, from the first idea of watching Arctic ice; the company's own timeline now lists a defence contract as its largest. Finnish understatement as the register: the press release notes which way north is, and calls the mission a full success "regarding our most important goals". The idea began as a university course project.

Humour: The tent on the roof (an early prototype pointed at the International Space Station, under a tent tied to scaffolding). The satellite that stayed up for years after the company had declared the mission over. Note the Polish exchange student and the Finnish friend, and the phrase "the first ever Finnish-Polish satellite". The re-entry of X1 with no recorded position. Never joke at any named person's expense.

Reflection: Things are declared finished long before they are gone, and the going is rarely recorded. Keep it to the X1 passage and let it sit.

Ending: Back in the present. Public catalogues still list X2 as in orbit; nobody has announced that it is finished. Quiet, a little amused, no moral.

Dark comedy dose: 3

Length: about 700 words.

## Fact sheet

Sources are listed at the end. Where two sources disagree, the sheet says so and the story must stay vague or use the safer figure.

Company and people
- ICEYE began in 2012 as a course project at Aalto University, in a joint course run by Aalto's business school and its technology university with Stanford professors lecturing on technology entrepreneurship. The Aalto Center for Entrepreneurship funded the team that year. The spin-off began operations in 2015. [Aalto, 2015 funding article; Aalto Entrepreneurship Society piece]
- Rafał Modrzewski, born in Poland in 1989, graduated from Warsaw University of Technology, came to Finland on the EU Erasmus exchange programme to study at Aalto, and stayed. At Aalto he led the data handling team for Finland's first nanosatellite, Aalto-1. [Poland Weekly; ICEYE history page]
- At Aalto he met Pekka Laurila. [ICEYE history page]
- His co-founder is Pekka Laurila, whom Poland Weekly calls his Finnish friend. Modrzewski is CEO; Laurila has been described as CFO and as CSO in different releases.
- The co-founders had worked on the team that built Finland's first satellite and left to found ICEYE before that satellite launched. [Trajectory magazine]
- The name comes from the company's first project, studying how climate change affects Arctic ice along the Northern Sea Route. [Trajectory; Poland Weekly]
- ICEYE's first contract was in 2015 with ExxonMobil, for Arctic ice detection. [SpaceNews, 2018]
- It took almost four years from the idea to a working satellite. [SpaceNews, Modrzewski]
- The company says the industry considered a SAR satellite under 100 kg impossible. Conventional SAR satellites typically weigh more than 1,000 kg. [ICEYE history page; ICEYE X1 press release]
- An early ICEYE prototype radar was set up on an office roof in Helsinki under a white tent tied to scaffolding and pointed at the International Space Station, and produced a clear microwave image of it. A True Ventures partner, writing in June 2026, says the tent looked ready to collapse in the slightest wind. The date of the experiment is not given. [True Ventures blog]
- ICEYE's own timeline lists a joint venture with Rheinmetall anchored by a Bundeswehr contract of about EUR 1.7 billion, described as the largest sovereign space reconnaissance contract in European history (a company claim). Wikipedia gives EUR 1.76 billion. The company also supported Ukraine from 2022. [ICEYE history page; Wikipedia]

ICEYE-X1
- Launched 12 January 2018, 03:59 UTC, as a secondary payload on ISRO's PSLV-C40 from Satish Dhawan Space Centre, Sriharikota, India. The primary payload was Cartosat-2F. [eoPortal]
- 70 kg (eoPortal gives conflicting figures; ICEYE's own interview material and Orbital Radar say 70 kg). Described as the first SAR satellite under 100 kg and Finland's first commercial satellite. [ICEYE; eoPortal; Orbital Radar]
- Began communicating with the ground at 05:20 UTC on 12 January. [ICEYE press release]
- First image: Noatak National Preserve, Alaska, taken Monday 15 January 2018 at 21:47 UTC. About 80 by 40 km, acquired in about ten seconds, more than 1.2 GB of raw data, resolution 10 by 10 metres. The press release notes north is roughly to the right of the image. Published 17 January 2018. [ICEYE press release]
- Modrzewski, in that release: the mission "is already a full success regarding our most important goals". [ICEYE press release]
- ICEYE announced on 29 June 2018 that the goals had been met and operations had concluded. More than 600 images were collected during the mission. [eoPortal; ICEYE X2 launch release]
- Re-entered the atmosphere on 10 February 2024 by natural decay, after 2,220 days in orbit. "No position was recorded for this re-entry." Single source. [Orbital Radar]

ICEYE-X2
- Launched 3 December 2018 on a SpaceX Falcon 9 (Block 5) from Vandenberg Air Force Base, California, as part of Spaceflight's SSO-A: SmallSat Express mission. [ICEYE releases; Gunter's Space Page]
- Communications were established at 19:58 CET, on launch day. [ICEYE launch release]
- ICEYE called it "the first ever Finnish-Polish satellite" and "the world's second SAR microsatellite". [ICEYE releases]
- Mass 80 kg and orbit about 570 by 587 km at 97.77 degrees inclination, sun-synchronous. [Gunter's Space Page; mass is not in ICEYE's releases]
- Improvements over X1 included a larger range of incidence angles and finer ground resolution (10 m to about 3 m), and propulsion for orbital adjustments. [ICEYE releases; SpaceNews]
- First image: taken at night on 7 December 2018, four days after launch, of the Aralar and Aizkorri-Aratz natural parks in the Basque Country, Spain. More than 500 square kilometres (about 20 by 25 km), 3 by 3 metre resolution, 2.4 GB of data downlinked for the single frame. Published 11 December. [ICEYE press release]
- Modrzewski, in the X2 release: "We are ecstatic to have had not one but two groundbreaking missions this year". [ICEYE press release]
- N2YO lists X2 (NORAD 43800) as not decayed. Whether it is still operating is not known. [N2YO]

Physics (general knowledge, consistent with ICEYE's own descriptions)
- SAR sends its own radio waves to the ground and builds an image from the energy that scatters back, so it can image day or night regardless of cloud. [ICEYE X1 press release]
- Synthetic aperture: the satellite's own motion lets a small antenna behave like a much larger one. In the True Ventures prototype the radar stood still and the ISS's motion supplied the change. [True Ventures blog]

Disputed or not to be used
- Wikipedia's constellation counts and some funding totals are inconsistent. Do not quote them.
- ICEYE-X1's mass is given as 70, 85 and under 100 kg in different places; use 70.
- Do not state that X2 is still working; only that public catalogues list it as in orbit.
- Do not quote Modrzewski's remarks about NASA tests or the "ultimate test"; the wording is unverified.
