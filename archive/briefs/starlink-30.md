# Brief: Starlink-30

Angle: The first sixty Starlinks, seen through the night they were launched: a sky full of lights that a UFO website could only describe with a question mark, a satellite built to be disposable, and a catalogue whose entry for it does not match the satellite. (Companion entry: starlink-31.)

Form: A single night examined from every side. Open on the Dutch reports of 25 May 2019, then widen to the launch, the release, the climb, the brightness complaint and the planned ending. Close on the question mark.

Weights: history 35, technology 30, culture 25, reflection 10.

Entry: Early on 25 May 2019, a Dutch UFO-reporting website receives more than 150 reports of a line of lights.

Technology to explain: How sixty satellites were released at once with no per-satellite mechanism (the rocket turns slowly, each satellite has slightly different rotational inertia, so they drift apart like a deck of cards spread on a table). How a krypton Hall thruster, which uses electricity to push gas out slowly, lifts them from about 440 km to about 550 km. Why a flat panel with a large solar array can be bright at the edge of night.

Culture: Spotters, astronomers and the observation that nobody owns the night sky. Musk's first and second responses to the brightness complaint; the January 2020 darker-coated satellite.

Humour: The question mark in "Faster than a plane. Huh?". Press kit promising that 95 per cent of each satellite burns up.

Reflection: Shared things change because they are on nobody's list. A satellite designed to end.

Ending: Return to the question mark.

Dark comedy dose: 3 (several dark turns, one admission, one tangent: people in a street looking up at nothing, one callback: the question mark).

Length: about 740 words.

## Fact sheet

Identity (catalogue check)
- Catalogue id "starlink-30" carries NORAD 44238. NORAD 44238 is STARLINK-24 (international designator 2019-029D), launched 24 May 2019 from Cape Canaveral, re-entered 24 Oct 2022 after 1,249 days, mass 227 kg, Starlink 0.9 programme. [orbitalradar.com/satellite/44238; isstracker.pl/en/satelity/44238 gives the same decay date 2022-10-24]
- The real STARLINK-30 is NORAD 44244 (2019-029K), re-entered 13 Oct 2020 after 508 days [orbitalradar.com/satellite/44244]; Aerospace Corporation predicts reentry 14 Oct 2020 22:06 UTC, +/-12 hours [aerospace.org/reentries/44244]. The sources disagree by about a day.
- Catalogue says launched "2019": correct for all four. Description "one of thousands": the launch batch was sixty; "thousands" is true of the constellation, not of this batch.

Launch and release
- Launch: Falcon 9 from Cape Canaveral, 10:30 p.m. EDT Thursday (0230 GMT Friday) 23/24 May 2019; satellites released at 11:32 p.m. EDT. [Spaceflight Now, 24 May 2019]
- 60 satellites, about 227 kg (500 lb) each, 13,620 kg total, heaviest payload SpaceX had lofted at that point. [Spaceflight Now, 24 May 2019]
- Released together in a clump; Musk: "So there's not actually a spring-based or specific deployment mechanism per satellite." The rocket rotated slowly and each satellite had slightly different rotational inertia; Musk compared the spread to "spreading a deck of cards on a table." [Spaceflight Now, 24 May 2019]
- Satellites were built in Redmond, near Seattle. [AFP via spacewar.com]
- Release orbit about 440 km; to raise themselves to about 550 km at 53 degrees. [Spaceflight Now]
- Musk said "it's possible that some of these satellites may not work." [Spaceflight Now]

Design
- Flat-panel design, multiple antennas, a single solar array; Hall thrusters using electricity and krypton gas. Krypton is cheaper than xenon but less thrust-efficient. [Spaceflight Now, 15 May 2019, citing SpaceX press kit]
- Press kit: "95 percent of all components of this design will quickly burn in Earth's atmosphere at the end of each satellite's life cycle." [Spaceflight Now]
- If a satellite fails at the lower orbit, SpaceX said atmospheric drag brings it down within about five years. [Spaceflight Now; Space.com 3 July 2019]
- By Jun 15 to Jul 7 2019 most had reached about 548 x 551 km. [satobs.org/seesat/Sep-2020/0055.html]

Sightings and brightness
- More than 150 sighting reports filed with the Dutch site ufomeldpunt.nl early Saturday 25 May 2019 (AFP). Spotter quote: "There's a long line of lights. Faster than a plane. Huh?" [spacewar.com, AFP]
- Satellites were "much brighter than SpaceX or astronomers anticipated"; likely because of large solar arrays reflecting sunlight. [Spaceflight Now 28 Apr 2020; MIT Technology Review]
- Musk first dismissed concerns, saying the constellation would not affect observations at all; he later said he had asked the team to look at reducing reflectivity. [MIT Technology Review]
- January 2020: one satellite with a darker coating; astronomer Pat Seitzer: "about a factor of two-and-a-half fainter", still visible to the naked eye in excellent conditions. [Spaceflight Now 28 Apr 2020]
- 28 June 2019: SpaceX said three satellites were no longer responding to commands; SpaceX had said beforehand they were experimental with higher risk of failure. [Space.com 3 July 2019]

## Disputed or not to be used
- Starlink-30 reentry date: 13 Oct 2020 (Orbital Radar) vs 14 Oct 2020 22:06 UTC +/-12 h (Aerospace). Say "mid-October".
- Launch date: 23 May (US evening) vs 24 May (UTC). Use "Thursday night" and the Dutch Saturday sightings.
- Cause of catalogue mismatch is unknown; do not state one. Why Starlink-24 lasted until 2022 is not sourced.
- Number of failed satellites later: sources differ (three or four). Use "three by the end of June".
- Not usable: any claim about which individual satellites were seen by whom; calls to police (not in source).
- Blocked or unreadable: celestrak.org, in-the-sky.org, keeptrack.space, Wikipedia launch list.
