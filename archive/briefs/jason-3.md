# Brief: Jason-3

Angle: A satellite that measures the sea to within centimetres, to catch a rise measured in millimetres a year, and the quiet handover of its job to a successor that spent months following it about.

Form: A handover. Open on the day the reference role passed to Sentinel-6 Michael Freilich, then walk through what was being handed over (the measurement itself), then follow Jason-3 into what it does afterwards.

Weights: technology 45, history 25, reflection 20, culture 10.

Entry: 7 April 2022. Sentinel-6 Michael Freilich takes over from Jason-3 as the reference for the height of the sea.

Technology to explain: Radar altimetry. Pulses sent straight down, echo timed, time turned to distance by the speed of light. Sea height is the satellite's own position minus the distance to the water, so both numbers must be known to centimetres: the range after correcting for water vapour and the charged upper atmosphere, the position from ground beacons whose signal pitch shifts as it passes (Doppler). One exact image: a stopwatch fine enough to notice the echo from 1,336 km down and back is late by the width of a thumb's worth of centimetres. Keep to the sheet's figures.

Culture (light): The sea is not level. Gravity from dense rock on the seabed shifts the surface by tens of metres.

Humour: The handover as an office leaving do with no cake. The successor following behind, copying everything, until it agrees with the old one.

Reflection: The record matters, the instruments do not. A rise too small for any one reading is seen only by many years of readings. Say the unsaid thing: a job done properly is one nobody notices being replaced.

Ending: The marks are still climbing; Jason-3 itself is, by dated sources, still doing a quieter job.

Dark comedy dose: 3 (several dark turns, one admission, one tangent on a child's height pencilled on a door frame, one callback on the pencil)

Length: about 650 words.

## Fact sheet

Status and operations
- Sentinel-6 Michael Freilich became the altimetry reference mission on 7 April 2022, the fifth reference mission after Topex/Poseidon, Jason-1, Jason-2 and Jason-3. Both its redundant altimeters were calibrated against Jason-3. It followed Jason-3 about 30 seconds behind so both measured the same features. On 7 April Jason-3 was shifted to a new interleaved orbit; the interleaved orbit gave denser coverage from 25 April 2022. [AVISO, "Hand-over in space", April 2022]
- A second tandem phase with Sentinel-6A ran from 30 January to 2 June 2025. Jason-3 was moved into a drifting orbit from 18 June 2025. [CLS/AVISO Jason-3 2025 annual report executive summary, report dated 2 March 2026]
- NOAA notice of 17 June 2025: Jason-3 moved to a Long Repeat Orbit; affected data (OGDR) were interrupted from 2 June 2025 to about 18 June 2025, about two weeks; Poseidon-3B altimeter was put in standby during the move. [NOAA OSPO messages 20250528_1822 and 20250617_1637]
- Plan stated in 2023: the long repeat orbit is "approximately 27 km below the reference orbit", chosen to serve geodetic science and operations. This is a 2023 plan, not confirmed as executed. [OSTST 2023 OPE discussion topics, aviso]
- 14 November 2025: NASA states "Jason-3 and Sentinel-6 Michael Freilich remain active"; Jason-1 and Jason-2 have been decommissioned. Sentinel-6B will eventually become the official reference satellite. [NASA Sentinel-6 blog, 14 Nov 2025]
- Calibration/validation report for cycle 637 (16 to 26 June 2026), issued 31 July 2026: Jason-3 has been in a Long Repeat Orbit since 18 June 2025; performing well despite instrument ageing; no major anomaly in 2025; a change from the long repeat orbit to an interleaved long repeat orbit begins in that cycle. [CLS BilanCalval J3 Cycle 637, aviso]
- No source found stating Jason-3 has been decommissioned. No source found for a completed handover to Sentinel-6B.

Mission
- Launched 17 January 2016, 10:42 a.m. PST, on a SpaceX Falcon 9 from Vandenberg Air Force Base, California. The mission is led by NOAA with NASA, France's CNES and EUMETSAT; CNES provided the spacecraft bus. [JPL news release, "Jason-3 launches to monitor global sea level rise"]
- The Falcon 9 first stage attempted to land on a barge; one of the four legs failed to lock out and the rocket toppled over. [satellitetoday.com, 19 Jan 2016; JPL]
- Orbit: 1,336 km altitude, 66.04 degrees inclination, repeats every 9.9156 days (127 revolutions); orbital speed 7.2 km/s. [NASA Earthdata Jason-3 platform page]
- Covers about 95 per cent of the ice-free ocean every 10 days. [JPL news release]
- Instruments: Poseidon-3B radar altimeter, AMR-2 microwave radiometer (measures water vapour), DORIS, GPS receiver, Laser Retroreflector Array. [NOAA NESDIS Jason-3 spacecraft page; JPL mission page]
- Jason-3 was designed for 5 years. [eoPortal]

How radar altimetry works
- An altimeter sends radar pulses toward the sea and times the echo; there are over 1700 pulses per second. The round-trip time converts to distance by the speed of light; averaged over a second, and after correcting for water vapour and ionisation which slow the signal, the range is known "within 2 cm". [AVISO, basic principle]
- The satellite's altitude above the reference ellipsoid is known to within 3 cm. DORIS measures the Doppler shift of signals from ground beacons to find velocity. Sea surface height = satellite position minus range. [AVISO, basic principle]
- The sea surface is not level: a dense seafloor rock zone could shift sea level by "tens of metres" (the geoid). [AVISO, basic principle]
- Sea surface height varies by about 2 to 3 metres from place to place. [NASA Earth Observatory, "Taking a measure of sea level rise"]
- Poseidon-3B is nadir-looking (straight down). [eoPortal Jason-3]

Sea level
- In 2024 global sea level rose 0.59 cm (0.23 inches) per year, against 0.43 cm expected; mostly because seawater expands as it warms. The rate has more than doubled over three decades. [JPL image PIA26189, 13 March 2025]

Check on catalogue description ("millimetre precision")
- Not supported. Single-range accuracy is about 2 cm and the Jason-3 crossover sea surface height error was reported as about 3.27 cm in 2025 [CLS 2025 executive summary]. Millimetres belong to the rate of rise per year, not the measurement. "The oceans are rising" is supported (above). The description also omits that its reference role passed in 2022.
