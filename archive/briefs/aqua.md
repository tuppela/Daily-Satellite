# Brief: Aqua

Angle: A satellite built to keep one appointment, half past one in the afternoon, which it kept for years and then, for lack of fuel, began to miss by a little more every day. One number examined from every side.

Form: A single number examined from every side: 1:30. Open on it, come back to it at the end changed (the appointment kept, then the appointment missed, then earned lateness). Between: what the afternoon is for (AIRS, the sounder that feeds weather forecasts), the drift, the wear, the status.

Weights: technology 30, history 30, culture 15, reflection 25.

Entry: "Half past one in the afternoon. That was the appointment."

Technology to explain: AIRS. It collects infrared energy from below, splits it into wavelengths, and 2,378 detectors each read one wavelength; each wavelength responds to temperature and water vapour across its own range of heights, so the air is read like a tall building, floor by floor. Combined, this gives a temperature profile with accuracy NASA calls comparable to a weather balloon. Weakness: thick cloud blocks infrared.

Culture (light): Weather forecasts as an everyday annoyance: the coat carried for nothing, the correct forecast that is invisible.

Humour: It was named for the time of day first (EOS PM) and for water second. It launched at 2:55 in the morning to be an afternoon satellite. It left the A-Train it was first into. A door built for 3,022 movements has made more than 43,000, so its use was reduced.

Reflection: Design life six years, 24th year of data in May 2026. Nobody applauds the thing that arrives on time. Keep the unsaid thing to one lone paragraph.

Ending: Return to half past one. Kept from 2002 until 2021. Earned being late.

Dark comedy dose: 3

Length: about 800 words.

## Fact sheet

Identity and launch
- Aqua is a NASA Earth science satellite, part of the Earth Observing System. "Aqua" is Latin for water; its objective is to study Earth's water cycle. [science.nasa.gov/missions/aqua, page modified 5 Oct 2026]
- It was formerly named EOS PM, for its afternoon equator crossing time. [science.nasa.gov/missions/aqua; aqua.gsfc.nasa.gov]
- It measures evaporation, water vapour, clouds, precipitation, soil moisture, sea ice, land ice, snow cover, radiative energy fluxes, aerosols, vegetation, phytoplankton and dissolved organic matter, and air, land and water temperatures. [science.nasa.gov/missions/aqua]
- Launch: 4 May 2002, 2:55 a.m. PDT, from SLC-2W, Vandenberg Air Force Base, California, on a two-stage Delta II 7920-10L. [science.nasa.gov/mission/aqua/about-aqua-spacecraft-and-instruments]
- Design life six years. [science.nasa.gov/missions/aqua; aqua.gsfc.nasa.gov]
- On 4 May 2026 NASA marked its 24th year of data collection, with over 126,000 orbits and over 30,000 publications supported. [aqua.gsfc.nasa.gov, last updated 3 Sept 2026]
- Orbit as designed: sun-synchronous, near-polar, 705 km, inclination 98.2 degrees, period 98.8 minutes, equatorial crossing 1:30 p.m. at the ascending node. [science.nasa.gov about-aqua-spacecraft-and-instruments]
- It was the first satellite launched into the Afternoon Constellation, the A-Train. By early 2022 CloudSat, CALIPSO and Aqua had left it. [aqua.gsfc.nasa.gov, 3 Sept 2026]

Instruments
- Six Earth-observing instruments: AIRS, AMSU-A, HSB, AMSR-E, MODIS, CERES. Four (AIRS, AMSU, CERES, MODIS) still produce high-quality data. [science.nasa.gov/missions/aqua, 5 Oct 2026]
- HSB (Humidity Sounder for Brazil) collected about nine months of high-quality data before failing in February 2003. [science.nasa.gov/missions/aqua]
- AMSR-E had a major anomaly in October 2011, sent reduced-quality data for intercalibration, and was powered off in March 2016. [science.nasa.gov/missions/aqua]
- AIRS: built by BAE Systems for NASA/JPL. Infrared energy from the ground is directed into the instrument by a scan mirror; the optics separate it into wavelengths (NASA offers a raindrops-splitting-sunlight analogy and notes the technique differs). 2,378 infrared detectors, each sensing one wavelength; earlier space instruments had 15. Each wavelength responds to temperature and water vapour across a range of altitudes from the surface to the stratosphere. Combined readings give a temperature profile (a "sounding"); NASA says accuracy is comparable to weather balloon measurements. Thick clouds block the infrared; microwave instruments see through cloud with limited accuracy and an algorithm combines both. [JPL "How AIRS Works", archived at webarchive.library.unt.edu]
- AIRS and forecasts: a 2008 NASA article says AIRS improves forecast models by improving the data fed into them, giving "the best picture of the vertical temperature and moisture structures ever made from space"; weather balloons are launched twice a day and sample the lowest 7 to 10 miles of the atmosphere; a graduate student had learned to improve 48-hour forecasts by 3 hours (date of that result not given). [phys.org, 21 July 2008]
- MODIS solar diffuser door has moved 43,042 times (May 2025 slide) or 43,222 times (Aug 2025 slide) against a design limit of 3,022; its use was reduced. Three of four 10 W calibration lamps have failed. [Aqua status slides, May 2025 and 8 Aug 2025]

Spacecraft and orbit drift
- Spacecraft bus rated "Excellent Health"; 33 of 132 solar array strings failed (99 remain, 75 per cent), as of 8 Aug 2025. [aqua.nasa.gov/sites/default/files/AquaStatus.pdf, as of 8 Aug 2025]
- Northward equatorial crossing stayed between 13:30 and 13:45 mean local time from May 2002 until maneuvers completed 18 March 2021 (target 13:35). [Aqua status slides, 2025]
- Last drag makeup maneuver December 2021, because of limited fuel. Since then "free-drift": slowly descending below the A-Train, drifting to later crossing times and lower altitudes. Free-drift exit from the A-Train began January 2022. [science.nasa.gov/missions/aqua; status slides]
- The 13:45 upper science limit was exceeded in February 2023. Predicted crossing about 15:30 in July 2026 (prediction in the August 2025 slides). Perigee-lowering manoeuvres planned for September and October 2026. [status slides, 8 Aug 2025]
- No numeric current altitude is given on any NASA page I could read (charts only).

Status
- NASA's Aqua mission page, modified 5 Oct 2026, lists it as an "Active Mission" with four instruments producing high-quality data. [science.nasa.gov/missions/aqua]
- NASA's Aqua site, last updated 3 Sept 2026: no current systems issues; "a strong chance of operating successfully into September 2026". [aqua.gsfc.nasa.gov]
- A projection in the 8 Aug 2025 status slides, assuming funding: instrument shutdown and end of science 26 Aug 2026, followed by passivation; arrays supply minimum bus power until 21 Sept 2027. No source found confirming whether the shutdown happened. [status slides]
- 29 Dec 2025 report: NASA confirmed plans to retire Terra, Aqua and Aura, "possibly as early as the end of 2026". [satnews.com]
- 3 Feb 2026 report: NASA sought industry and academic input on partnerships to operate one or more of the three; projected operational end dates Terra February 2027, Aqua September 2027, Aura July 2028. [militaryaerospace.com]
- Earthdata alert (2 May 2025): Terra and Aqua will "begin shutting down operations" in "late 2026/early 2027". [earthdata.nasa.gov]
- I could not confirm the orbit's actual state from a live tracker (celestrak blocked).

Disputed or not to be used
- MODIS door count: 43,042 vs 43,222. Story says only "more than forty-three thousand".
- Passivation date: 4 Nov 2026 (May 2025 slides) vs 11 Nov 2026 (Aug 2025 slides). Not used.
- End dates differ across sources (26 Aug 2026 instrument shutdown; "into September 2026"; end of 2026; September 2027 projected end). Story says only that a funding-dependent projection existed and that no source confirms what happened.
- Thomasnet (Oct 2024) says HSB failed "in early 2023"; NASA says February 2003. NASA used. Thomasnet also contains an internal error on Aura. Not used.
- Earthdata feature article on VIIRS gives a "10:15 a.m." lower limit and a September 2023 exit date that conflict with NASA status slides. Not used.
