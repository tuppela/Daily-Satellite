In the early hours of Saturday the twenty-fifth of May, 2019, a Dutch website for reporting UFOs received more than a hundred and fifty reports.

They were all about the same thing. A long line of lights, crossing the sky. One of the spotters put it like this: "There's a long line of lights. Faster than a plane. Huh?"

I have read a great many official statements about space, and that question mark is the most honest thing in any of them.

It was sixty satellites. Same make, about two hundred and twenty-seven kilograms each. They had left Florida on the Thursday night at half past ten, Florida time, and been let go about an hour later.

Let go is the right phrase. There was no spring for each one, no little door. The rocket turned slowly, each satellite resisted the turning in a slightly different way, and the whole clump came apart on its own. Elon Musk compared it to spreading a deck of cards on a table.

Each one was a flat panel with a single solar wing and a thruster that ran on krypton. The thruster does not kick. It uses electricity to push gas out of the back, very gently, for a very long time, the way you get up a hill by never stopping rather than by running. They started at about four hundred and forty kilometres and climbed to about five hundred and fifty under their own power, which most of them had done by early July.

And here is the part nobody had planned. The wing is flat and it is large, and at the edge of the night, when the ground is dark and the satellites are still in sunlight, a large flat thing can throw light at you. They were much brighter than SpaceX or the astronomers had expected. The best guess at the time was the solar array.

You know what happens when one person in a street stops and looks up. Not at anything. Just up. The next person slows down, because nobody walks past that without checking. Within a minute there are six of you, all looking at the same empty piece of afternoon, each assuming one of the others knows. Nobody asks. Asking would mean admitting you joined late.

A hundred and fifty of them wrote it down.

The astronomers were less amused. Musk first said the constellation would not affect observations at all, and later said he had asked the team to look at making the satellites less reflective. In January 2020 one flew with a darker coating, and an astronomer put it at about two and a half times fainter. Still visible to the naked eye, in excellent conditions. It is hard to hide anything in the one place everyone looks when they have nothing else to look at.

I'd like to say my first feeling was solidarity with the astronomers. It was delight. A line of lights over the horizon is a lovely thing, and I would have gone outside, and I would have told someone, and I would have been very pleased with myself for knowing what it was.

Nobody owns the night sky, which means nobody was ever the person you had to ask.

That is how most shared things get changed. Nobody takes them. They are just not on anyone's list.

The satellites were built to be disposable, and said so. Before launch SpaceX called them experimental, with a higher risk of failing in orbit, and Musk said some might not work. By the end of June, three had stopped answering commands. The plan for a dead one was air: at that height, drag would bring it down in about five years. The press kit said ninety-five per cent of the components would burn up on the way. It is an odd thing to put in a brochure about the future. Most of this will be gone, and we are aiming to do better on the last five.

As for the two this entry is filed under, the archive has given them a name and a number that do not belong together. I will own up to that in the other entry. For now, whichever of them you meant, they have come down.

I think about that question mark more than I should. Most of what we do about the sky starts with it, and then someone fetches a longer word.
