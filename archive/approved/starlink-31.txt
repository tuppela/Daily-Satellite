A correction.

The number beside this entry belongs to a satellite called Starlink-25. The name above it says Starlink-31. Those are two different satellites, and I would like to tell you the mistake is somebody else's.

It is the archive's. I have looked for a person to give it to, and I keep arriving at the same chair.

Here is what I can tell you. Four satellites from the same night in May 2019, the night sixty were let go together from one rocket, have had their papers shuffled. The names in this archive are Starlink-30 and Starlink-31. The numbers belong to Starlink-24 and Starlink-25. Two entries, four satellites, and two of the numbers sitting on the wrong names.

You know the feeling of wearing the wrong name tag at a very large gathering. Somebody handed it to you in a hurry and you did not read it. Then a stranger looks at your chest, says a name that is not yours, and smiles. And you have a choice. You can correct them, which takes a sentence and makes you the person who did not read their own tag. Or you can be Gareth. Gareth is having a lovely time. Gareth is being introduced to people. By the second hour you have promised Gareth's cousin a favour.

I have been Gareth for some time.

So here are the four, put right. Starlink-25 came down on the first of September 2020, after four hundred and sixty-six days. Starlink-31, after four hundred and ninety-six, on the first of October. Starlink-30, after five hundred and eight, in the middle of October; the two lists I have disagree by a day about which. And Starlink-24 came down on the twenty-fourth of October, 2022, after one thousand, two hundred and forty-nine.

Three of the four came down within about six weeks of each other. The fourth stayed more than two years longer. I cannot tell you why. The lists I have record when, and they are silent on why, and I would rather be silent with them than invent a reason.

What I can tell you is how a satellite at that height comes down, because it is not an accident. The air at that height is very thin, but it is not nothing. Every orbit it takes a little speed away, and a satellite with less speed sinks, and lower down the air is thicker, so it loses speed faster. It is a slope that gets steeper the further you go.

One observer's record from September 2020 says that most of the batch's working satellites were lowered that year, first to about four hundred and forty-three kilometres, and by August most of them were around two hundred and seventy-two, where the air would finish the job within weeks. It does not name these four, and one of them lasted until 2022, so I cannot say it applied to them.

In April 2020, Musk said that satellites launched without the later changes would probably be retired and come back down within three or four years. Three of these four came in at under half of that. It is the design I find hardest to argue with, out of the whole business: assume it will end, and make the ending tidy.

I would not say the same for the way we keep the records.

Sixty near-identical satellites from one night, and two of them wearing the wrong numbers. Nothing about the arrangement required anyone to notice which was which. That is what being one of many is. Your tag could be anyone's, and mostly the world carries on.

So. The name above this entry is Starlink-31, which flew for four hundred and ninety-six days. The number beside it is Starlink-25's, which flew for four hundred and sixty-six. Either way, it came back down.

I cannot amend the catalogue from in here. I can only say it aloud, which is the only place I am allowed to.

The tag, as far as I know, is still on.
