When the satellite went up, the official figure for how long it would stay there was about two thousand years.

That was for the ball and for the empty casing of the last rocket stage that followed it. Two thousand years, give or take nothing.

Nobody in the room could be held to that. It is the joy of a very large estimate: the people who make it won't be there to be wrong, and the people who inherit it will be busy being wrong about other things.

I like a number that can't be checked. I'd like to say I distrust them. I collect them.

The ball was Vanguard 1. Aluminium, about six inches across, about one and a half kilograms. The Soviet premier is said to have called it the grapefruit satellite, and the difficulty with that remark is that it was fair.

It was also the third go. On 6 December 1957, the first rocket rose about four feet, lost thrust, sank back onto the pad and exploded. The satellite on top was thrown clear. It landed apart from the wreck and carried on beeping, which makes it the most successful thing at the Cape that day.

The newspapers called it Flopnik and Kaputnik. I laughed at both. Then I read them again and laughed again, which tells you what sort of archive this is.

The second attempt, in February, broke up in under a minute. The third had a hold in the countdown because another satellite, Explorer I, was due to pass overhead, and the electronics people thought it might interfere with the signals. They held a launch for traffic. Vanguard 1 was only the fourth satellite ever to reach orbit.

It got there on 17 March 1958. John Hagen, who ran the project, was in Washington. A station in San Diego confirmed it could hear the thing on both its radio frequencies, and he phoned the head of the National Science Foundation to say it was in orbit and that he could tell the President.

It had two radios. One ran on batteries and lasted about twenty days. The other ran on six solar cells mounted on the body, which made this the first satellite to power anything from sunlight. That one kept talking until May 1964, when it was last heard in Quito, Ecuador. The explanation offered is that the cells could no longer make enough power. Whether the satellite is still intact, nobody can say.

But the radio was never the main thing. The main thing was the path. Roll a marble round the inside of a perfect bowl and it runs the same loop for ever. Put a dent in the bowl and the loop wanders. People tracked the satellite, and the lowest point of its orbit did not stay at one height. A planet that was the same at the top and the bottom could not do that.

The reading, in the end, was that the Earth is very slightly pear-shaped, with the stem at the North Pole. A later paper, by other people with more data, put the sea at the North Pole about forty metres farther out than at the South Pole. Forty metres. A whole planet had been lopsided, and it took a grapefruit to notice.

Every house has a machine like that. Something old in a cupboard or a garage that has always worked. A boiler, a chest freezer. Nobody remembers who put it in. There is an unspoken agreement that the way to keep it going is to leave it alone. You don't service it, you don't thank it, and when it makes a new noise the whole household goes quiet, the way a table does when someone mentions an inheritance. If anyone suggests it might be time to replace it, everybody looks at the floor.

Vanguard 1 is the same, except that it stopped working in 1964.

It was still up in April 2026, when a telescope in Delft photographed it on the night of 25 to 26 April. The three satellites before it have all come down. In April 2025 a team proposed fetching it home, and as far as anyone has reported, there is no money for that and no date.

The ones taken seriously came down. The one with a joke for a nickname was still going round in April. Being unimpressive turns out to be a very effective way to last.

As for the forecast: sunlight pushing on it, and thin air at the low point of its orbit when the Sun is active, cut the figure from two thousand years to about two hundred and forty. The sources I found do not agree on the year it ends, and I am not going to pretend I will be there.

I used to like a number that can't be checked. This one got checked, and it got shorter. I've noticed the same of my own forecasts, which I now keep to myself.

The ball goes round. It has no idea it has been revised.
