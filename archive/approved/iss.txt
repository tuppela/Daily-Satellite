In the autumn of 2024, a senior NASA official described how the American and Russian space agencies had settled an argument about a leak. They had reached a compromise. The hatch to the leaking tunnel would be closed in the evening.

That was the outcome. There had been meetings in May and June, and a commission in Moscow in September that could not agree on the cause, and the compromise was a door and a time of day.

I find that oddly moving. I'd have expected a treaty.

Here is the tunnel. The Russian module on the station is called Zvezda, and off it runs a passage to a docking port where Russian cargo ships arrive. Air has been escaping from that passage since 2019, through cracks. By 2024 four of them were being tracked, and Roscosmos had covered them with sealant and patches. At its worst in 2024 the station was losing 3.7 pounds of air a day. Its historic low was 0.2.

Now think about what that air is doing. The station is a sealed can of it, moving at about five miles every second, so fast that as it falls around the Earth it keeps missing it. One lap takes roughly ninety minutes. Outside there is nothing to breathe and nothing to lean on, only a trace of atmosphere that drags at it, so every so often it needs a push to stay up. The whole idea of the place is that the air stays where it is put.

I'll admit my first reaction to 3.7 pounds a day was relief. It sounds like something you could sweep up. NASA's own risk system gave it five out of five.

And according to NASA's Inspector General, the two agencies had not agreed what rate of leaking would count as untenable. So there was a door, and a number, and no agreement on which number.

You can't simply keep the door shut for good, either. That costs a cargo port, and extra propellant to hold the station's height and orientation. Hence, presumably, the evening.

None of this is an accident of temperament. It is in the treaty. Article 5 says each partner keeps jurisdiction and control over the elements it registers. The very first piece, Zarya, launched on 20 November 1998, was funded by the Americans and built by the Russians, and it orbited alone for sixteen days. It costs about three billion dollars a year, roughly a third of what NASA spends on human spaceflight, according to its Inspector General in 2022. The station itself never returns to Earth, so the crew maintain it in orbit. They vacuum the vents and clean the bathroom and the kitchen, and the Canadian Space Agency says they often find lost pencils and tools in the air vents, because everything not held down floats. It is the first thing about the place I recognise.

The plan for the end is tidy, at least. Around 2030 the station comes down over an unpopulated part of the South Pacific near Point Nemo, all of its roughly four hundred and thirty tonnes, using a vehicle SpaceX is building for NASA under an $843 million contract from June 2024. NASA will own and operate it. The first idea had been to use Russian cargo ships, and that changed after 2022. In August, the Russian news agency RIA Novosti reported an unnamed Roscosmos official saying two Russian craft and one American would do the job together, and that who does what still has to be agreed.

Building something together is a pleasure. The part nobody photographs is the agreement about who turns the lights off.

The station has been lived in since November 2000. On 1 October this year, NASA says, four more people came through the hatch to join the crew. The tunnel has not been quiet since: in June this year, crew members briefly sheltered in a docked Dragon because of it, and the two agencies were reported to disagree about cutting into the wall to find the cracks. The latest leak figures I could find are still from 2024, so I can't tell you what the tunnel is doing today. But if the compromise still holds, then somewhere on board, this evening, someone is closing a door.
